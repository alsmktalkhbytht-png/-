Introduction: What Are Trichomonads?
INTRODUCTION
• Trichomonads are unicellular eukaryotic protozoa belonging to the group
Parabasalia.
• They typically move using flagella; some possess an undulating membrane that
assists motility.
• The recognized human-associated species are known primarily as trophozoites; no
true cyst stage is known for the main huma n species discussed here.
• Clinical relevance depends on the species and its site: the urogenital tract, oral
cavity, or intestine.
• The genus name Trichomonas has been used broadly in older literature, so current
taxonomic names should be checked.

Species Covered in This Presentation
TAXONOMIC NOTE
• Trichomonas vaginalis: a sexually transmitted urogenital pathogen.
• Trichomonas tenax: primarily an oral trichomonad associated with poor oral health
and periodontal disease; its causal role is not fully established.
• Pentatrichomonas hominis (historically called Trichomonas hominis): an intestinal
trichomonad generally considered nonpathogenic or of uncertain clinical
significance.
• Trichomonas gallinae: an important paras ite of birds, included for taxonomic
comparison rather than as a routine human parasite.
• The main clinical focus is on the three species associated with humans.

General Characteristics
GENERAL FEATURES
• Unicellular organisms; the trophozoite is the recognized form in the main human-
associated species.
• Reproduction occurs by longitudinal binary fission.
• Flagella are used for motility; the number of flagella and extent of the undulating
membrane vary by species.
• No resistant cyst stage is known for the main human species; transmission
generally occurs through direct contact or suitable moist secretions.
• Motility in wet preparations is affected by specimen freshness, transport conditions,
and temperature.

1. Trichomonas vaginalis — Introduction and Medical Importance
SPECIES 1
• T. vaginalis is the most clinically important
human trichomonad and causes trichomoniasis.
• It infects the urogenital tract: the vagina, cervix,
and urethra in women, and the urethra and
sometimes prostate in men.
• Transmission occurs mainly through sexual
contact, and infection may remain asymptom atic.
• The World Health Organization estimated
approximately 156 million new infections
worldwide in 2020 among people aged 15–49
years.
• Trichomoniasis is treatable, but reinfection is
common if sexual partners are not treated.

T. vaginalis — Morphology and General Features
SPECIES 1
• The trophozoite is pear-shaped or oval and is
approximately 7–30 µm long.
• It has one anterior nucleus, four anterior flagella,
and a fifth flagellum associated with the undulating
membrane.
• An axostyle extends toward the posterior end and
may project beyond it.
• Motility is rapid and jerky or tumbling; this can be a
helpful feature in a fresh wet mount.
• No true cyst stage is known, and the organism does
not survive well in the external environment.

T. vaginalis — Epidemiology and Risk Factors
SPECIES 1
• Infection occurs worldwide, with prevalence varying by population, age group, and
diagnostic method.
• Risk is increased by factors such as multiple sexual partners and other sexually
transmitted infections.
• Many infected women and men have no symptoms; absence of symptoms does not
exclude infection.
• Transmission through swimming pools or toilet seats is not considered the usual or
epidemiologically established route.
• Assessment for other sexually transmitted infections may be appropriate according
to the clinical context.

T. vaginalis — Pathogenesis and Clinical Features
SPECIES 1
• The parasite adheres to urogenital epithelial cells and can cause cellular injury and
local inflammation.
• In women, symptoms may include vaginal discharge, itching, erythema, dysuria, or
pain during intercourse; infection may also be asymptomatic.
• Vaginal pH may be above 4.5, but this finding is nonspecific and cannot establish
the diagnosis.
• In men, infection is often asymptomatic; u rethritis, discharge, dysuria, and
occasionally prostatitis may occur.
• Trichomoniasis is associated with increased risk of HIV acquisition and some
adverse reproductive outcomes; these associations do not mean every infection
causes such outcomes.

T. vaginalis — Life Cycle
SPECIES 1
• The trophozoite is the recognized form in
humans; no cyst stage is known.
• The parasite is transmitted mainly during
sexual contact through infected genital
secretions.
• It colonizes the urogenital tract and
multiplies by longitudinal binary fission.
• Trophozoites may be present in genital
secretions or urine and can be passed to a
sexual partner during contact.
• Both the infective and diagnostic forms are
trophozoites; cysts are not expected in
specimens.

T. vaginalis — Laboratory Diagnosis: Specimens
SPECIES 1
• For women, a vaginal swab or vaginal discharge is
a preferred specimen; other specimens may be used
according to the validated test.
• For men, urine or a urethral swab may be used;
specimen choice depends on the test platform.
• Wet mount: a fresh specimen is mixed wit h saline
and examined promptly for motile trophozoites.
• Examination should be performed quickly because
wet-mount sensitivity decreases substantially as
motility is lost.
• Collect and transport specimens according to
laboratory and assay instructions, while maintaining
privacy and informed consent.

T. vaginalis — Laboratory Diagnosis: Methods and Interpretation
SPECIES 1
• NAAT/PCR methods are among the most sensitive options and are preferred when a
validated test is available for the specimen type.
• Wet mount is rapid and inexpensive, but its sensitivity is limited; a negative result
does not rule out infection.
• Culture in a specialized medium may be used in selected situations, including
persistent infection or drug-susceptibility a ssessment.
• Rapid antigen tests are available for certa in specimens and populations; follow the
approved indications of the specific test.
• A Pap smear is not a definitive diagnostic test. If organisms are incidentally
observed, confirmation with an appropriate sensitive test is recommended.

T. vaginalis — Prevention and Treatment Overview
SPECIES 1
• Effective treatment generally uses nitroimidazoles such as metronidazole or
tinidazole, according to clinical guidelines.
• Sexual partners should be treated, and sexual contact should be avoided until
treatment is completed and symptoms have resolved as advised.
• Retesting women at approximately 3 months is recommended because reinfection is
common.
• Self-medication or unsupervised topical tr eatment is not recommended; regimen
selection belongs to a qualified healthcare professional.
• This slide is educational and is not an individualized prescription.

2. Trichomonas tenax — Introduction and Habitat
SPECIES 2
• T. tenax is a trichomonad found mainly in the oral cavity, especially
dental plaque and periodontal pockets.
• Its presence is often associated with poor oral hygiene, gingivitis,
and periodontal disease.
• Detection alone does not prove that the organism caused the
disease; it may colonize an already diseased oral environment.
• Rare reports describe it at extraoral sites, but the se are not the
typical presentation and do not establish causality in every case.
• It is not considered a sexually transmitted infection
• Transmission through saliva or shared items contaminated with oral
secretions is plausible, but the importance of each route is not
precisely defined.
• The moist oral environment, dental plaque, and periodontal pockets
may support its persistence.

T. tenax — Morphology and General Features
SPECIES 2
• It exists as a motile trophozoite; no cyst stage is known.
• It has anterior flagella and an undulating membrane, but
morphology may overlap with that of other trichomonads.
• Species identification based on morphology alone may
be difficult, particularly in suboptimal specimens.
• It inhabits moist oral environments and may be detected
in plaque or periodontal-pocket specimens using suitable
methods.
• Molecular testing may be needed for precise
identification in research or selected cases.

T. tenax — Epidemiology and Pathogenicity
SPECIES 2
• Detection rates vary with oral health, the population studied, specimen collection,
and diagnostic method.
• It may be found more often in people with periodontal disease or poor oral hygiene.
• Its direct causal role in gingival inflammation or tissue destruction has not been
fully established.
• Rare reports describe detection in respiratory specimens or abscesses; these
findings should be interpreted cautiously.
• There are no symptoms specific to T. tenax alone; clinical findings are usually
related to the underlying oral condition.

T. tenax — Laboratory Diagnosis
SPECIES 2
• Specimens may include dental plaque, periodontal-pocket material, or other
appropriate oral samples, depending on the purpose of testing.
• Direct microscopy of a fresh sample may reveal motile trophozoites, but
morphology may not reliably establish the species.
• Culture or molecular testing may be used in research laboratories or when species
confirmation is required.
• Detection must be distinguished from pro of of causation: finding the organism does
not by itself establish that it caused periodontal disease.
• Clinical management focuses on assessment and treatment of oral disease and
dental hygiene by an appropriate professional.

3. Pentatrichomonas hominis — Name and Significance
SPECIES 3
• The organism is commonly called Pentatrichomonas hominis; older literature may
refer to it as Trichomonas hominis.
• It is an intestinal trichomonad found in the colon and passed in feces.
• It is generally considered nonpathogenic, although a possible role in diarrhea
remains debated and other pathogens may be present at the same time.
• Diarrhea should not automatically be attributed to this organism without
investigating other causes of intestinal infe ction.
• When teaching or reviewing literature, provide both the current name and the
historical name when useful.

P. hominis — Morphology and General Features
SPECIES 3
• The trophozoite is elongated or pear-shaped and moves
using flagella.
• It has anterior flagella and an undulating membrane;
detailed morphology should be checked against the
diagnostic reference used.
• No cyst stage is known; the form observed in stool is
the trophozoite.
• It may resemble other trichomonads, so morphology
must be interpreted alongside specimen origin and other
microscopic features.
• Trophozoites are relatively fragile; fresh stool is
preferred when motility is being assessed.

P. hominis — Epidemiology and Pathogenicity
SPECIES 3
• It is reported worldwide, with detection rates varying by sanitation, hygiene, and
diagnostic methods.
• Transmission is presumed to occur mainly by the fecal–oral route through
contaminated hands, food, or water.
• Most references consider it a commensal or an organism of uncertain/low
pathogenicity; a consistent causal relations hip with diarrhea has not been
established.
• When gastrointestinal symptoms are present, evaluate for better-established causes
such as Giardia, Entamoeba histolytica, and relevant bacterial or viral pathogens.
• It is not usually regarded as an invasive pathogen and does not require a complex
life cycle or intermediate host.

P. hominis — Life Cycle
SPECIES 3
• Trophozoites are passed in the feces of colonized individuals.
• Transmission is presumed to occur when fecally contaminated material is ingested.
• The organism reaches and inhabits the lumen of the large intestine.
• It reproduces by longitudinal binary fission, and trophozoites are passed again in
stool.
• No resistant cyst stage is known, which likely limits environmental survival
compared with parasites that form resistant cysts.

P. hominis — Laboratory Diagnosis
SPECIES 3
• Specimen: fresh stool; prompt examination is
preferred when motility is being assessed.
• Direct wet-mount microscopy may reveal
motile trophozoites, but sensitivity depends on
timing and specimen quality.
• Concentration methods or permanent stains
may help clarify morphology according to the
laboratory protocol.
• Species-level identification can be difficult by
microscopy alone; molecular tests may help in
research or unclear cases.
• Detection in stool does not prove that P.
hominis caused symptoms; correlate with the
clinical picture and investigate other
pathogens.

Laboratory Comparison of the Main Species
COMPARISON
• T. vaginalis: urogenital tract; specimens include vaginal secretions/swabs or suitable urine
and genital specimens; NAAT is often the preferred confirmatory method.
• T. tenax: oral cavity and periodontal pockets; specimens include dental plaque or gingival
material; microscopy or molecular testing may be used depending on purpose.
• P. hominis: colon; specimen is fresh stool; direct microscopy may be used, with cautious
interpretation of clinical significance.
• Specimen source is essential for differentiation; motility or the name alone is insufficient.

Key Differences in Life Cycle and Transmission
COMPARISON
• T. vaginalis: primarily sexual transmission; urogenital colonization; no known cyst
stage.
• T. tenax: associated with the oral cavity; transmission through saliva or oral
secretions is plausible; no known cyst stage.
• P. hominis: intestinal habitat; presumed fecal–oral transmission; no known cyst
stage.
• All species discussed reproduce by binary fission, but their habitats and
transmission routes differ.

Common Diagnostic Pitfalls
LABORATORY PEARLS
• Do not treat a negative wet mount as definitive exclusion of T. vaginalis; sensitivity
is lower than that of molecular tests.
• Do not delay wet-mount examination and then rely on absent motility to exclude the
organism.
• Detection of T. tenax does not prove that it caused periodontal disease.
• Do not attribute diarrhea to P. hominis without considering other intestinal
pathogens.
• Do not confuse the historical name Trichomonas hominis with the current name
Pentatrichomonas hominis.

Conclusion
TAKE-HOME MESSAGES
• T. vaginalis is the most clinically important species and causes a treatable sexually
transmitted urogenital infection.
• T. tenax is associated with the oral cavity and periodontal disease, but its causal role
is not established in every case.
• P. hominis is an intestinal trichomonad generally considered nonpathogenic or of
