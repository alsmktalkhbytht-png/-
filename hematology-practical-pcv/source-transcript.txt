Determination Of Packed Cell Volume (PCV) or  Hematocrit 
Presented By : Linda Munjed Hadi
=== SLIDE 2
 INTRODUCTION
Packed Cell Volume (PCV), or hematocrit, is the percentage of whole blood occupied by red blood cells.
It can be measured in capillary, venous, or arterial blood.
PCV is commonly used as part of the complete blood count to help detect anemia, polycythemia, and changes in plasma volume.
=== SLIDE 3
Introduction 
 PCV indicates how much of the blood volume is occupied by cells.
 A PCV of 50% means that 50 mL of packed cells are present in 100 mL of blood.
 PCV may rise when the number of red blood cells increases or when plasma volume decreases, as in dehydration.
=== SLIDE 4
• PCV is the percentage of packed red blood cells in the total blood column.
It is affected mainly by red blood cell mass and plasma volume.
Clinically, it helps detect anemia, polycythemia, hemoconcentration, and hemodilution.
Increased hematocrit may reflect hemoconcentration; decreased hematocrit may reflect hemodilution.
Markedly high hematocrit may be associated with increased thrombotic risk.
=== SLIDE 5
  Principle Of The Test 
When anticoagulated blood is centrifuged at a suitable speed and time, it separates into three layers:
• Bottom layer: packed red blood cells
• Middle thin layer: buffy coat (white blood cells and platelets)
• Top layer: plasma
=== SLIDE 6
  Normal value
Males: 40–54%
Females: 36–47%
Newborns: 55–68%
=== SLIDE 7
Methods 
There are two main methods used to determine hematocrit:
1. Microhematocrit (manual method)
2. Electronic method
=== SLIDE 8
 Microhaematocrit
The microhematocrit method requires only a small amount of blood (about 2–3 drops).
Blood may be collected by finger puncture and drawn into a capillary tube by capillary action.
=== SLIDE 9
MATERIALS & INSTRUMENTS
1. Blood specimen
2. Capillary tube
3. Microhematocrit centrifuge
4. Hematocrit reader
5. Clay or sealing material
=== SLIDE 10
Procedure 
Fill two capillary tubes with blood to about ¾ of their length.
2. Seal one end of each tube with clay or wax (about 2 mm deep).
3. Place the tubes exactly opposite each other in the microhematocrit centrifuge.
4. Centrifuge at about 10,000 rpm for 5 minutes.
5. Remove the tubes carefully and read the hematocrit using a card reader.
=== SLIDE 11
 Card Reader
To read the result:
• Place the capillary tube vertically on the hematocrit reader.
• Align the bottom of the packed red cell column with the 0% line.
• Align the top of the plasma column with the 100% line.
• Read the hematocrit at the top of the packed red cell layer.

=== SLIDE 14
                      Medical applications                  
PCV is influenced by red blood cell mass, red blood cell shape, and plasma volume.
PCV may increase in:
• Polycythemia
• Dehydration
• Burns
PCV may decrease in:
• Anemia
• Pregnancy
• Kidney or liver disease
=== SLIDE 15
 Medical Condition
Reduced plasma volume may occur with dehydration, diuretic use, or fluid loss.
Increased red cell mass may be:
• Primary: polycythemia
• Secondary: chronic lung disease, smoking, high altitude, or some tumors
=== SLIDE 16
 Sources of error  
1. Excess anticoagulant may shrink red blood cells and lower the hematocrit reading.
2. Poor sealing of the capillary tube may cause blood loss during centrifugation and a falsely low result.
3. Forcing the centrifuge to stop can disturb the packed red cell layer and give a falsely high result.
