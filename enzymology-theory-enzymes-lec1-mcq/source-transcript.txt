1- Enzymes: are protein catalysts (and occasionally RNA called ribozymes) similar to chemical catalyst such as Pt or Fe+2, that increases the rate of reactions without itself being consumed. Enzymes has a molecular weight in the range of 12,000 - 1,000,000.
Enzymes act with high specificity on one or a class of particular substances called substrates. Enzymes differ from chemical catalyst in their following properties:
a. They have a high degree of specificity for their substrates; therefore no side reaction occurs, so no wasteful by-products are produced.
b. They accelerate chemical reactions tremendously.
c. They function in aqueous solutions under very mild conditions of pressure, temperature and pH, and this rare in non biological catalysts.

2. Nomenclature and Classification of Enzymes:
Each enzyme is assigned two names, recommended name and systematic name.
a. Recommended name: Enzyme are names related to the substrate that act on it with adding the suffix "-ase" (for example, glucosidase, urease, sucrase), or names according to the nature of enzymatic reaction (for example, lactate dehydrogenase and adenylyl cyclase). There are some difficulties for this name, because some enzymes take names which not indicate on their acts, for example, trypsin and pepsin, for this reason a systematic name is suggested.
b. Systematic name: Developed by the International Union of Biochemistry and Molecular Biology (IUBMB). The most important of this system was followed:
 1. Enzymes are divided into six major classes. Within each class are subclasses, and under each subclass are sub-subclasses.
 2. Enzyme name consist of two part, the first refer to the substrate (s), and the second refer to the nature of enzymatic reaction end with the suffix (-ase).
 3. If additional information requires for clarifying the nature of reaction, the previous information was followed and put it in parentheses. For example: Recommended = Malic enzyme; Systematic = L-Malate: NAD oxidoreductase (decarboxylating) — substrate | reaction nature | reaction occur by removing carboxyle group as CO2.
 4. For each enzyme there is a systematic code number consist of four numbers and a sign E.C. (Enzyme Code):
   - First number represents the major class of enzyme, which clarifies the nature of reaction.
   - Second number represents the subclass of enzyme which specifies the group or bond related for catalyst.
   - Third number represents the sub-subclass of enzyme which refers for example to the compound that accepted the electrons in oxidoreductase reactions.
   - Fourth number represents the serial listing of the enzyme in its sub-subclasses, which give randomly.

Systematic Classification of Enzymes According to the Enzyme Commission (six major classes):
1. Oxidoreductases (1.1 acting on C-OH as e- donor: 1.1.1 with NAD(P) as acceptor, 1.1.3 with O2; 1.2 acting on C=O; 1.3 acting on CH-CH)
2. Transferases (2.1 transferring C1 groups: methyl, hydroxymethyl/formyl, carboxyl/carbamoyl; 2.2 aldehydes/ketones; 2.3 acyl; 2.4 glycosyl; 2.6 N groups: amino transferases; 2.7 P groups: kinases)
3. Hydrolases (3.1 hydrolyse ester linkages: carboxylic ester, phosphoric monoester, phosphoric diester hydrolases)
4. Lyases (addition to double bonds) (4.1 C=C lyases: carboxy, aldehyde; 4.2 C=O lyases; 4.3 C=N lyases: ammonia lyases)
5. Isomerases (isomerization reactions) (5.1 racemases and epimerases; 5.2 cis-trans isomerases)
6. Ligases (ATP-dependent bond formation) (6.1 C-O; 6.2 C-S; 6.3 C-N; 6.4 C-C: carboxylases)

Example reactions:
1. Oxidoreductases: Pyruvate + NADH + H+ -> L-Lactate + NAD+  (lactate dehydrogenase)
2. Transferases: Glucose + ATP -> Glucose 6-phosphate + ADP  (hexokinase)
3. Hydrolases: Maltose + H2O -> Glucose + Glucose  (maltase)
4. Lyases: Fumarate + H2O -> L-malate  (fumarase)
5. Isomerases: 3-phosphoglycerate <=> 2-phosphoglycerate  (phosphoglyceromutase)
6. Ligases: Pyruvate + CO2 + ATP -> Oxaloacetate + ADP + P + H+  (pyruvate carboxylase, 6.4.1.1)

3. PROPERTIES OF ENZYMES:
a. Active sites: Enzyme molecules contain a specific pocket or cleft of amino acid side chains that create a three-dimensional surface complementary to the substrate. Two major hypotheses suggested explaining the active site function:
 - Lock and key hypothesis: Proposed by Fischer, that the active site of the enzyme it is complementary in shape to that of the substrate.
 - Induced-Fit Hypothesis: Proposed by Koshland: a. The enzymes change shape upon binding substrate. b. The active site has a shape complementary to that of the substrate only after the substrate is bound.
b. Catalytic efficiency: Most enzyme-catalyzed reactions are highly efficient, proceeding from 10^3 to 10^8 times faster than uncatalyzed reactions. Each enzyme molecule is capable of transforming 100 to 1000 substrate molecules into product each second.
c. Specificity: Enzymes are highly specific, interacting either with one substrate (absolute specificity) like urease enzyme which is the hydrolyze urea only, or act on a specified mode of groups or bonds (group specificity) like trypsin enzyme which is catalyzing of the peptide bonds hydrolysis on the carboxyl side of lysine and arginine residues only.
d. Regulation: Enzyme activity can be regulated, that is, enzymes can be activated or inhibited, so that the rate of product formation responds to the needs of the cell.
e. Cofactors: Some enzymes associate with a nonprotein cofactor that is needed for enzymatic activity. Commonly encountered cofactors include:
 1. Organic molecules:
   a. Coenzymes: are often derivatives of vitamins that are weakly bind to the active site of enzyme. For example, the coenzyme NAD+ contains niacin, FAD contains riboflavin, and coenzyme A contains pantothenic acid.
   b. Prosthetic groups: Is tightly bound coenzyme that does not dissociate from the enzyme. For example, the biotin.
 2. Metal ions: such as Zn+2 or Fe+2. These ions may be act in different mechanism such as:
   1. Essential components of enzyme active site.
   2. May be act as a bridge connects the substrate to enzyme to form coordinate complexes.
   3. Can be stabilized the enzyme conformation by interact its charge with the opposite charge of enzyme.
 - Holoenzyme refers to the enzyme with its cofactor. Apoenzyme refers to the protein portion of the holoenzyme. In the absence of the appropriate cofactor, the apoenzyme typically does not show biologic activity. Holoenzyme = Apoenzyme + Cofactor.

4. Enzyme Units:
a. International Unit (IU): The amount of enzyme required to catalyze the conversion of one micromole of substrate per minute under optimum condition.
b. Turnover number: The number of substrate molecules converted to product per second by single enzyme molecule.
c. Specific Activity: The number of international units of enzyme present in 1 mg of protein.
d. Katal (kat): The amount of enzymatic activity that converts one mole of substrate per second. 1 kat = 6 x 10^7 U.

5. Enzyme Kinetics: Is a branch of enzymology study the factors that effecting on the enzymatic reaction velocity. The most important factors are: a. Temperature. b. pH. c. Enzyme concentration. d. Substrate concentration.
Factors effecting enzyme activity:
 1. Effect of temperature: The rate of an enzyme-catalyzed reaction increases when the temperature increased to limited degree as a result of increasing kinetic energy of reaction molecules then decrease when the temperature increasing over this degree as a result of there denaturation.
 2. Effect of pH: The pH at which maximal enzyme activity is achieved that differs for different enzymes. Each of these curves distinguished by optimum pH (pepsin, trypsin, alkaline phosphatase). Decreasing enzyme activity on both side of pH can be explained by: 1. denaturation of the enzyme, because the structure of the catalytically active protein molecule depends on the ionic character of the amino acid side chains, therefore the enzyme loss its activity. 2. The catalytic process usually requires that the enzyme and substrate have specific chemical groups either an ionized or non ionized state in order to interact, or this not happen only in limited pH.
 3. Enzyme concentration: The rate of an enzyme-catalyzed reaction increases with the enzyme concentration, but not more than substrate concentration.
 4. Substrate concentration: The rate of an enzyme-catalyzed reaction increases with substrate concentration increased until a maximal velocity is reached. The more increasing of substrate concentrations not effect on the reaction rate of enzyme, which is reflects the saturation with substrate of all available binding sites on the enzyme molecules present.
