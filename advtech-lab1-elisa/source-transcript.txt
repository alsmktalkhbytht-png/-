What Is ELISA?

ELISA is a plate-based immunoassay used to detect or quantify a specific antigen or antibody using specific immune binding and an enzyme-generated measurable signal.

Solid phase: usually a 96-well microplate.

Recognition: specific antigen–antibody interaction.

Detection: enzyme-linked system.

Readout: colour intensity measured as optical density (OD).

2. Qualitative or Quantitative?

QUALITATIVE ELISA

Final result: positive / negative (reactive / non-reactive).

Usually uses positive + negative controls and a kit-defined cut-off or S/CO.

No exact concentration.

QUANTITATIVE ELISA

Final result: concentration.

Uses standards with known concentrations → standard curve → calculate sample concentration.

Example units: pg/mL, ng/mL, IU/mL.

A kit does not have to contain standards + positive/negative controls together.

Direct ELISA is used to detect the presence of antigens in a sample with speed and convenience. The process involves coating an ELISA plate with samples containing potential antigens, then adding a primary antibody that is enzyme-conjugated.

The difference in an Indirect ELISA is that the primary antibody is unlabeled and a secondary labeled antibody is used to bind to the primary antibody.

This antibody will bind directly to the antigen if present. After incubating, any unbound antibodies are washed away, leaving only the antigen-primary antibody complexes. Next, a substrate is added that reacts with the enzyme linked to the antibody, causing a color change. The intensity of this color is measured using a spectrophotometer, which indicates the quantity of antigen in the sample.

This type of assay offers greater flexibility because one set of secondary antibodies can be used to detect multiple primary antibodies. After incubating the plate with samples containing antigens and incubating with the primary antibody, labeled secondary antibodies are then added that bind to any attached primary antibody.

[Slide 13]

A Sandwich ELISA is used to detect specific proteins with high sensitivity and specificity.

First, the ELISA plate is coated with capture antibodies that bind to the target protein.

After introducing a sample, these antibodies grab onto any of the proteins present.

Following this step, detection antibodies are added on top of the bound protein, which are typically linked to an enzyme or have a tag that allows for visualization.

Next, a substrate is added into the mix, which the enzyme-linked antibody will react with to produce a measurable signal.

A Competitive ELISA holds a unique position. It operates on the principle where samples and control antigens compete for binding to a specific amount of capture antibody.

Preparation starts by coating an ELISA plate with these antibodies.

Then, a mixture of unlabeled antigen from the sample and labeled antigen is introduced to each well.

If high amounts of target protein are present in the sample, they will occupy more of the binding sites, leaving fewer sites for the labeled antigen.

During detection, only bound enzymes emit signals—higher target concentration results in weaker signals because fewer enzyme-conjugated antigens remain attached to antibodies.

This inverse relationship provides the ability to quantify amounts accurately.

6. Washing & Incubation — Why They Matter

WASHING

Removes unbound sample, excess conjugate and non-specific material.

Poor washing → high background / falsely high OD.

Harsh washing or dried wells → weak or inconsistent signal.

Follow the exact wash cycles in the kit.

INCUBATION

Gives binding reactions enough controlled time.

Why 37°C? Some assays are validated at 37°C for efficient, reproducible reaction kinetics.

37°C is NOT universal. Use the exact kit time and temperature.

9. Reading & Interpretation

Microplate reader

Measures OD at the kit-specified wavelength.

Avoid bubbles and read within the required time.

Quantitative

Standards → standard curve → convert sample OD to concentration.

Qualitative

Validate controls → calculate cut-off/S/CO → classify positive or negative.

10. Common Errors + Quick Checklist

COMMON ERRORS

• Wrong pipetting → wrong OD

• Poor washing → high background

• Unequal incubation → variability

• Wrong temperature → altered kinetics

• Wells dry → irregular signal

• Delay after stop → unreliable reading

BEFORE STARTING

✓ Qualitative or quantitative?

✓ Read kit IFU + plate map

✓ Prepare reagents correctly

✓ Label wells

✓ Set time + temperature

✓ Prepare wash buffer + reader

✓ Never mix instructions from different kits
