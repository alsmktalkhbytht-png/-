**Advanced Clinical Biochemistry (Practical)**

## Introduction

Liver function tests measure various chemicals in the blood made by the liver. Abnormal results indicate a problem with the liver and may help to identify the cause.

## Major functions of the liver

1. Bile production and excretion.
2. Excretion of bilirubin, cholesterol, hormones, and drugs.
3. Metabolism of fats, proteins, and carbohydrates.
4. Enzyme activation.
5. Storage of glycogen, vitamins, and minerals.
6. Synthesis of plasma proteins, such as albumin, and clotting factors.

## Main tests for liver function

- Alanine Aminotransferase (ALT), also called Glutamic-Pyruvic Transaminase (GPT).
- Aspartate Aminotransferase (AST), also called Glutamic-Oxaloacetic Transaminase (GOT).
- Serum Bilirubin (total, direct, and indirect).
- Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP).
- Albumin, total protein, etc.

## Alanine Aminotransferase (ALT) / Glutamic-Pyruvic Transaminase (GPT)

ALT enzyme catalyzes a reversible transamination reaction: the transfer of an amino group from L-alanine to α-ketoglutarate to produce pyruvate and L-glutamate.

**[Image 01 — Page 2: ALT reaction diagram]**

This test is primarily used to diagnose liver disease and to monitor the course of treatment for hepatitis, active post-necrotic cirrhosis, and the effects of later drug therapy.

It is an enzyme found primarily in the liver and kidney. Normally, low levels of ALT exist in serum. This enzyme level increases with liver damage and is used for screening and/or monitoring liver disease.

ALT is found in plasma and in various body tissues but is most commonly associated with the liver. High concentrations occur in the liver, and relatively low concentrations are found in the heart, muscle, and kidney.

- ALT is more specific than AST for liver disease, but AST is more sensitive to alcoholic liver disease.
- ALT also differentiates between hemolytic jaundice and jaundice due to liver disease.

### Reference values

- Adult males: 10–40 U/L
- Adult females: 7–35 U/L
- Newborns: 13–45 U/L

### Clinical significance of ALT

Increased ALT levels are found in the following conditions:

1. Hepatocellular disease.
2. Alcoholic cirrhosis.
3. Obstructive jaundice or biliary obstruction.
4. Viral, infectious, or toxic hepatitis.
5. Myocardial infarction, heart failure.
6. Severe burns.
7. Obesity.

## Aspartate Aminotransferase (AST) / Glutamic-Oxaloacetic Transaminase (GOT)

AST catalyzes the reversible reaction converting aspartate and α-ketoglutarate to oxaloacetate and glutamate. Aspartate donates its amino group to form oxaloacetate; α-ketoglutarate accepts the amino group to form glutamate.

This reaction requires the coenzyme pyridoxine phosphate (a derivative of vitamin B6).

**[Image 03 — Page 5: AST reaction diagram]**

GOT is an enzyme present in tissues of high metabolic activity. The source document lists GOT in the heart, liver, skeletal muscle, kidney, brain, pancreas, spleen, and lungs.

The enzyme is released into the circulation following injury or death of cells. Any disease that causes changes in these highly metabolic tissues will result in a rise in GOT levels.

- This test is used to evaluate liver and heart disease. GOT is usually ordered along with GPT.
- This enzyme has two names: Aspartate Aminotransferase (AST) and Glutamic-Oxaloacetic Transaminase (GOT).

### Reference value

**5–45 U/L**

### Clinical significance

Increased AST levels occur in:

1. Myocardial infarction (MI).
2. Liver diseases (acute hepatitis, chronic hepatitis, alcoholic hepatitis, etc.).
3. Hypothyroidism.
4. Trauma and irradiation of skeletal muscle.
5. Hemolytic anemia, exhaustion, heat stroke, etc.
