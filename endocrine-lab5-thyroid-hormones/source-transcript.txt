THYROID HORMONES
Synthesis, Metabolism and Clinical Biochemistry

1. Thyroid Hormone Synthesis
Thyroid hormones are synthesized by follicular cells and stored in the colloid as part of
thyroglobulin.

Steps of Synthesis
1. Iodide trapping

              Iodide (I⁻) is transported from the blood into follicular cells by the sodium–iodide
               symporter (NIS).

2. Oxidation

              Thyroid peroxidase (TPO), using hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), oxidizes iodide into
               reactive iodine.

3. Organification

              Iodine binds to tyrosine residues within thyroglobulin, forming:
                  o MIT: Monoiodotyrosine (one iodine atom).
                  o DIT: Diiodotyrosine (two iodine atoms).

4. Coupling
The thyroid peroxidase enzyme catalyzes the coupling reactions:

              MIT + DIT → T3 (Triiodothyronine)
              DIT + DIT → T4 (Thyroxine)

5. Storage and release

              T3 and T4 remain attached to thyroglobulin in the colloid until needed.
              TSH stimulates colloid endocytosis and lysosomal proteolysis, releasing T3 and T4
               into the bloodstream.
              MIT and DIT are deiodinated within the thyroid cells, allowing iodine recycling.

High-yield concept: The thyroid gland secretes predominantly T4, while much of
circulating T3 is generated by peripheral conversion of T4.

2. Iodine: The Essential Element
Iodine is essential for the synthesis of T3 and T4.

              Recommended adult intake: approximately 150 µg/day.
              Chronic iodine deficiency may cause goiter and hypothyroidism.
              Excess iodine can temporarily inhibit thyroid hormone synthesis through the
               Wolff–Chaikoff effect.

Clinical application: Iodine-containing medications and contrast agents may alter thyroid
function, particularly in susceptible individuals.

3. T4 Metabolism
T4 acts as a circulating reservoir for the more biologically active T3.

Peripheral deiodinases convert T4 into different metabolites:

         Reaction               Product          Biological activity
Outer-ring deiodination  T3                     Active
Inner-ring deiodination  Reverse T3 (rT3)       Inactive
Other metabolic pathways Conjugated metabolites Facilitate elimination

T3 is generally more biologically active than T4 because it interacts more strongly with
thyroid hormone receptors.

Clinical pearl: T3 concentration and activity depend not only on thyroid secretion but
also on peripheral conversion.

4. Transport in Blood
More than 99% of circulating thyroid hormones are bound to plasma proteins.

        Carrier protein                                                                                                    Main characteristic
Thyroxine -binding globulin (TBG)                                                                                             Major high-affinity carrier
Transthyretin (TTR)                                                                          Also known as thyroxine -binding prealbumin
Albumin                                                                                      Lower affinity but high transport capacity

Only a small proportion circulates as free hormone.

              FT4: Free thyroxine.
              FT3: Free triiodothyronine.

These free fractions are available to tissues and are important in assessing thyroid
function.

Clinical application: Pregnancy, estrogen exposure, liver disease and some medications
can alter thyroid hormone-binding proteins. Consequently, total T4 may change without a
corresponding change in free T4.

5. Physiological Actions of Thyroid Hormones
A. Metabolic effects

Thyroid hormones increase basal metabolic rate, oxygen consumption and heat
production.

B. Carbohydrate metabolism

They increase glucose turnover and influence intestinal glucose absorption, hepatic
glucose production and peripheral utilization.

C. Lipid metabolism

They stimulate lipid turnover and promote hepatic LDL clearance.

Laboratory association: Hypothyroidism commonly increases total cholesterol and
LDL-C, partly because LDL clearance is reduced.

D. Protein metabolism

Physiological concentrations support growth and protein synthesis, whereas excessive
thyroid hormone promotes protein breakdown and muscle wasting.

E. Growth and development
Thyroid hormones are essential for skeletal maturation, normal childhood growth and
brain development.

Clinical pearl: Untreated congenital hypothyroidism can cause severe and potentially
irreversible neurodevelopmental impairment.

F. Nervous and reproductive systems

Thyroid hormones support normal cognitive function and reproductive health.
Hypothyroidism may be associated with poor concentration, menstrual irregularities,
infertility and pregnancy complications.

6. Thyroid Function Tests (TFTs)
TSH and FT4 are the principal tests used to assess thyroid function.

         Condition                      TSH                                                                                                                  FT4
Primary hypothyroidism                   ↑                                                                                                               ↓
Primary hyperthyroidism                  ↓                                                                                                               ↑
Subclinical hypothyroidism               ↑                                                                                                               Normal
Subclinical hyperthyroidism              ↓                                                                                                               Normal
Central hypothyroidism      Low or inappropriately normal                                                                                                ↓

FT3 is particularly useful when T3-predominant thyrotoxicosis is suspected.

Important distinction:

              Hyperthyroidism: Excessive synthesis and secretion of thyroid hormones by the
               thyroid gland.
              Thyrotoxicosis: Excessive thyroid hormone action, regardless of the source.

For example, thyroiditis and excessive thyroid hormone ingestion can cause thyrotoxicosis
without increased thyroid hormone synthesis.

7. Major Thyroid Disorders
Hypothyroidism

Insufficient thyroid hormone availability.

Common causes:
              Hashimoto thyroiditis.
              Iodine deficiency.
              Thyroidectomy or radioactive iodine treatment.
              Certain medications.
              Pituitary or hypothalamic dysfunction.

Hyperthyroidism

Excessive thyroid hormone synthesis and secretion.

Common causes:

              Graves disease.
              Toxic multinodular goiter.
              Toxic adenoma.

Hashimoto Thyroiditis

A chronic autoimmune disorder that damages thyroid tissue.

Laboratory clues: Elevated TSH in primary hypothyroidism; anti-TPO antibodies are
frequently positive.

Graves Disease

An autoimmune disorder in which antibodies stimulate the TSH receptor.

Laboratory clues: Suppressed TSH, elevated FT4 and/or FT3, and potentially positive
TSH-receptor antibodies (TRAb).

8. Normal Laboratory Values
Typical adult reference intervals for total thyroid hormones are approximately:

  Test                    Typical reference interval
Total T4                  4.5–11.2 µg/dL
Total T3                  60–180 ng/dL
TSH                       0.5–4.0 mIU/L

Reference intervals vary by laboratory, analytical method, age and pregnancy status. The
laboratory's own reference interval should always be used.
