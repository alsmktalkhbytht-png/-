INTRODUCTION
- Enzymes are protein catalysts that increase the rate or velocity of physiologic reactions in the body.
- Most enzymes are present in cells at much higher concentrations than in plasma.
- Measurement of their levels in plasma indicates whether their tissue of origin is damaged, leading to release of intracellular components into the blood.
- Clinical enzymology = measurement of enzyme activity for the diagnosis and treatment of diseases.

TYPES OF PLASMA ENZYMES
A. Enzymes that have definite physiological functions in the circulation (FUNCTIONAL): lipoprotein lipase; proenzymes of blood coagulation.
B. Enzymes that reflect organ pathophysiology (NON-FUNCTIONAL): normally extremely low in plasma vs high in tissues; in tissue damage released in greater amounts (diagnostic/prognostic). Include: enzymes in exocrine secretions (diffuse passively into circulation, e.g. amylase & lipase in pancreatic disorders); intracellular enzymes (detect organ disorders, e.g. MI and hepatitis).

Functional vs Non-functional (table):
- Concentration: functional higher in plasma than tissue ; non-functional very low in plasma vs tissue.
- Function: functional have known functions ; non-functional no known functions.
- Substrates: functional always present in blood ; non-functional absent from blood.
- Site of synthesis: functional = liver ; non-functional = different organs (liver, heart, brain, skeletal muscle).
- Effect of disease: functional decrease in liver diseases ; non-functional different enzymes increase in different organ diseases.
- Examples: functional = clotting factors (prothrombin), lipoprotein lipase, pseudo-cholinesterase ; non-functional = ALT, AST, CK, LDH, ALP, acid phosphatase, amylase.

DETECTION OF ENZYMES FOR DIAGNOSIS
- Liver Function Tests: SGOT, SGPT, ALP, GGT
- Cardiac Function Tests: CK-MB, Troponins
- Pancreatic Enzymes: Amylase, Lipase
- Muscle Enzymes: CK, LDH
- Bone Enzymes: ALP, ACP

ENZYMES FOR LIVER DISEASES
1. Markers of hepatocellular damage (normally inside hepatocytes, released on damage): ALT, AST.
2. Markers of cholestasis (primary membrane bound): ALP, GGT, 5-nucleotidase.

Aminotransferases/Transaminases: transfer an amino group from a 2-amino acid to a 2-keto acid. Sensitive liver indicators of liver cell injury; most helpful in acute hepatocellular disease such as hepatitis; released into blood when liver cell membrane damaged (increased permeability).
Types: AST = serum glutamic oxaloacetic transaminase (SGOT) ; ALT = serum glutamic pyruvic transaminase (SGPT).
- AST and ALT more important in assessing/monitoring liver cell inflammation and necrosis.
- In acute viral hepatitis: 100-1000 times increase in both ALT and AST, but ALT increased more than AST.
- According to AST/ALT ratio, estimate the extent of liver damage.
- Elevated plasma ALT more specific for liver disease than AST (AST may rise in MI, muscle necrosis, renal disorders).
- Aminotransferase levels > 1000 IU/L occur almost exclusively in extensive hepatocellular injury.
Normal: AST male <35 U/L, female <31 U/L ; ALT male <45 U/L, female <34 U/L.

MARKERS OF CHOLESTASIS
A. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP): group of enzymes that hydrolyse organic phosphates at high pH. High in osteoblasts of bone, cells of hepatobiliary tract, intestinal wall, renal tubules, placenta. Normal 0-45 IU/L. In adult, plasma ALP from bone and liver in ~equal proportions.
Isozymes of ALP: 1) Hepatic — rises in extrahepatic biliary obstruction. 2) Bone — osteoblastic activity; elevated in children during active growth. 3) Placental — rises in last 6 weeks of pregnancy. 4) Intestinal — rise after a fatty meal; may increase in GI disorders.
Causes of raised ALP: Physiological (pregnancy-placental; infants up to 5x-bone; children 2-5x pubertal growth). Pathological: Liver disease (intra/extrahepatic cholestasis; IBD/ulcerative colitis gut isoenzyme). Bone disease (rickets, osteomalacia; Paget's disease — may be very high; osteogenic sarcoma; hyperparathyroidism). Malignancy (bone/liver involvement or tumour production).

B. Gamma-glutamyl-transferase (GGT): catalyzes transfer of the γ-glutamyl group from peptides/compounds to an acceptor; involved in amino acid transport across membranes; found mainly in biliary ducts of liver, kidney, pancreas; increased in liver disease especially obstructive jaundice. Normal: male <55 U/L, female <38 U/L.
Causes of raised GGT: 1) Induction of enzyme synthesis without cell damage, by drugs or alcohol. 2) Hepatocellular damage (infectious hepatitis). 3) Cholestatic liver disease (GGT usually parallels ALP).

ENZYMES FOR PANCREATIC DISEASES
1. Amylase: hydrolase class, hydrolysis of 1,4-α-glycosidic linkages in polysaccharides. Low MW (54-62 kDa) — passes glomeruli; only plasma enzyme physiologically found in urine. Derived from pancreas and salivary glands. Mainly for diagnosis of acute pancreatitis. Normal 28-100 U/L.
Causes of raised amylase: 1) Marked increase (5-10x URL): acute pancreatitis, severe glomerular impairment. 2) Moderate increase (up to 5x): acute abdominal disorders (acute cholecystitis, perforated peptic ulcer, intestinal obstruction, salivary gland disorders e.g. mumps, salivary calculi).
2. Lipase: single-chain glycoprotein, MW 48 kDa; derived from pancreas but more specific for pancreatic pathology than amylase; longer half-life than amylase (more useful in acute pancreatitis and pancreatic carcinoma). Serum amylase rises in mumps/pancreatic/other; lipase rises only in pancreatitis. Determining both helps diagnose acute pancreatitis. Normal 40-200 U/L.
3. Trypsin: serine proteinase; hydrolyzes peptide bonds formed by carboxyl groups of lysine/arginine with other amino acids. Normal 25 ± 5.3 µg/L. Increased in pancreatic disease but limited clinical value.

ENZYMES FOR SKELETAL MUSCLE DISEASES
1. Total creatine kinase (CK): CK-MM marked increase; CK-MB less marked increase. 2. LDH-5.
A. Creatine Kinase (CK/CPK): found primarily in heart and skeletal muscle, lesser in brain, NOT in liver and kidney. Catalyzes transfer of phosphate between creatine and ATP/ADP; Creatine + ATP -> phosphocreatine + ADP; energy reservoir. Normal total CK: male 46-171 U/L, female 34-145 U/L.
Causes of raised CK: Physiological (highest in infancy/childhood 7-10y). Pathological (muscle damage marker): myocardial infarction, rhabdomyolysis, muscular dystrophy, autoimmune myositis, acute kidney injury.
Isoenzymes of CK: two subunits M (muscle) and B (brain) -> three isoenzymes: CK1 (CK-BB) brain; CK2 (CK-MB) mostly cardiac muscle; CK3 (CK-MM) both skeletal and cardiac.
CK-MB: more specific for cardiac muscle; more sensitive marker of myocardial injury than total CK; ~35% of cardiac CK, <5% of skeletal CK; always high after MI.
CK-BB: high in brain/after brain damage; may increase during parturition (childbirth).

B. Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH): enzyme that produces energy; catalyses reversible interconversion of lactate and pyruvate (a dehydrogenase transfers a hydride). Found in cardiac/skeletal muscle, liver, kidney, brain, erythrocytes. Plasma total LDH = non-specific marker of cell damage. Normal 140-280 u/l. Increased in MI, acute leukaemias, generalized carcinomatosis, acute hepatitis. Isoenzymes more useful to distinguish hepatic disease from MI.
LDH isoenzymes (tetramer, 4 subunits H=Heart or M=Muscle):
- LD1 (H4): heart, RBC, kidney.
- LD2 (H3M): reticuloendothelial system.
- LD3 (H2M2): lungs.
- LD4 (HM3): kidneys, placenta, pancreas.
- LD5 (M4): liver and skeletal muscle.
Elevation: LD1 — MI, megaloblastic anaemia, renal infarction. LD2/LD3 — acute leukaemia; LD3 main isoenzyme in malignancy of many tissues. LD5 — liver or skeletal muscle damage.
Causes of raised total LDH: 1) Artefactual (in vitro haemolysis or delayed separation of plasma). 2) Marked increase (>5-10x URL): circulatory failure with shock and hypoxia, MI, haematological disorders (megaloblastic anaemia, acute leukaemias, lymphomas). 3) Moderate increase (<5x): viral hepatitis, malignancy of any tissue, skeletal muscle disease, pulmonary embolism.

ENZYMES FOR MYOCARDIAL INFARCTION
Enzyme assays for acute MI: 1) CK ; 2) CK-MB isoenzyme ; 3) LDH ; 4) AST.
1. CK/CPK: phosphocreatine = energy reserve during muscle contraction; detects myocardial and skeletal muscle damage; M and B chains; three isoenzymes: CK-BB (brain, absent in blood), CK-MB (cardiac, N <6% of total CK), CK-MM (skeletal, N >95%). Reference total CK: males up to 180 U/L, females 25-40% lower. ↑total CK: myocardial damage, skeletal muscle damage, muscular exercise, drugs (digoxin, lidocaine). ↑CK-MB: myocardial damage.
2. AST: significantly elevated in acute MI. Normal 0-41 IU/L at 37°C. In acute MI serum activity rises sharply within first 12 hours, peak at 24 hours or over, returns to normal within 3-5 days. Rise depends on extent of infarction; >350 IU/L due to massive infarction (fatal).
3. LDH: present in almost all tissues; catalyzes reversible conversion of pyruvate and lactate; most often measured in MI. Normal 55-140 IU/L at 30°C. Time sequence after MI: ↑ after 12-24 hours, peak after 3-5 days, return to normal after 7-12 days. Magnitude proportional to extent of infarction.

ENZYMES FOR PROSTATE DISEASES
Acid Phosphatase (ACP): include all phosphatases that hydrolyze phosphate esters with optimum pH < 7.0. Produced primarily in prostate gland; also in erythrocytes, platelets, leukocytes, bone marrow, bone, liver, spleen, kidney, intestine. Normal 1.5-4.5 U/L.
Clinical: primarily to diagnose prostate cancer and monitor treatment; ACP replaced by PSA (more specific/sensitive). Non-prostatic causes: hemolysis, Paget disease, hyperparathyroidism with skeletal involvement, malignant bone invasion by cancers.

ENZYMES FOR BONE DISEASES
ALP: rises in rickets, osteomalacia, hyperparathyroidism, Paget's disease; also in primary and secondary malignancies of bones.
ACP: highly increased in bony metastasis of carcinoma of the prostate.
