Subphylum: Mastigophora (Flagellates)
Flagellates are unicellular microorganisms. Their locomotion is by lashing a
tail – like appendage called a flagellum or flagella and reproduction is by
simple binary fission.
Important species infecting humans include: Intestinal flagellates: Giardia lamblia, Chilomastix mesnili, Trichomonas
hominis
Genital flagellates: Trichomonas vaginalis
Oral flagellate: Trichomonas tenax
Tissue and blood flagellates: Leishmania tropica, L. braziliensis, L.
donovani, Trypanosoma rhodesiense, T. gambiense and T. cruzi

Parasitic flagellates
Flagellates constitute the largest group of parasitic protozoa.
Typically, the body of a flagellate is elongated and slender with a single
flagellum. However, some species are spheroid in shape, possess more than a
single flagellum, or lack flagella entirely.
The flagellum, which arises from a basal body or kinetoplast, may originate
near, and extend freely from, the anterior end,
Or it may run along the free margin as an undulating membrane attached to
the side of the organism.
In some case the flagellum passes through the entire body, extending beyond
the anterior end of the organism as a free structure.
Flagellates of the Digestive and Reproductive passages
Intestinal flagellates found in human are:
1. Giardia lamblia (duodenum, jejunum).
2. Trichomonas vaginalis (vagina, urethra).
3. Trichomonas tenax (mouth).

Giardia lamblia
(causes Giardiasis / lambliasis)
Host: Giardia lamblia life cycle in one host, human.
Infective form: Mature cyst.
Mode of transmission: Man acquires infection by ingestion of food and water
contaminated with mature cysts or rarely by sexual route (mainly in
homosexuals).
Giardia lamblia - Is the most common flagellate of the human digestive tract- 
cause of parasitic gastrointestinal disease.
The parasite is cosmopolitan (worldwide distribution).
Giardiasis, is more commonly found in children than in adults, and in
individuals residing in warm climates rather than in temporal climates (cold
climates);

It is estimated that 20,000 cases of giardiasis occur each year in the U.S., and
there is a 20% to 40% prevalence in the world's population.
Giardiasis is the most frequent protozoan intestinal disease and the most
common identified cause of water – borne disease associated with breakdown
of water purification systems, drinking from contaminated streams, travel to
endemic areas and day care centers.
Humans are the only important reservoir of the infection. Morphology:
In its life cycle, the pathogen Giardia lamblia exists in two forms, an active
form called a trophozoite, and an inactive form called a cyst.
Trophozoite
The trophozoites are confined essentially to the duodenum, but occasionally
invade the bile ducts.
Giardia Trophozoite is “tear – drop shaped” with a convex dorsal surface and
a concave ventral surface (adhesive disc) which makes contact with intestinal
mucosa.
The trophozoite possesses two nuclei, and four pairs of flagella.
Infection of new hosts occurs when mature cysts are ingested with
contaminated food or water. Following excystation in the duodenum, the
tetra-nucleate parasite undergoes cytokinesis forming two binucleate
daughter trophozoites, which then adhere to epithelial cells and feed.
Both cysts and trophozoites can be found in the feces (diagnostic stages).
In heavy infections, the intestinal mucosa may be carpeted with the parasites.
A single diarrheic stool from a heavy infected individual may contain several
billion parasites.
The flagellates penetrate into mucosal cells and also interfere with the
absorption of fat, and fat- soluble vitamins.
Heavy infections may be characterized by extensive ulcerations of the
intestinal mucosa.
Biliary disease sometimes occurs when flagellates pass up the bile duct.
The trophozoites multiply by longitudinal binary fission in the small intestine
and eventually encyst.
The cyst contains two; and the mature cyst tetra nucleate (four nuclei)
situated at one end. Cysts are passed in stools and remain viable in soil and
water for several weeks.

Life cycle
The flagellates have a simple, direct life cycle that involves no intermediate
hosts.
Both of the species included Giardia lamblia and Chilomastix mesnili, have
both the trophozoite and the cyst stage. Both stages are passed in feces, but
the trophozoites disintegrate after leaving the body so only the cyst is
infective.
Trichomonas species, however, occur only as trophozoites which therefore
serve as both the diagnostic and the infective stage
In the flagellates, size, shape, and structural details such as the flagella,
presence of an undulating membrane, or prominent cytostome are used more
often than nuclear detail for species identification.
Unlike the amebae, the shapes of both trophozoites and cyst of a given
species tend to be static and thus may serve for differentiation.
The trophozoites bodies are flexible but retain a characteristic shape, usually
elongate or“ pear – shaped”.
All of the flagellate, as the name implies, are equipped with flagella, the fibril
that serve as organ of motion. And each species is characterized by a definite
number and arrangement of these flagella and by a particular type of motion.
As with amebae, the intestinal flagellates are transmitted by direct contact or in contaminated food and drink. Trichomonas vaginalis, a Urogenital
parasite, is transmitted either by contact with contaminated articles or by
sexual intercourse.
Giardia lamblia is the only protozoa species that lives in the small intestine
rather than the large intestine.
Also, it is the only species that is bilaterally symmetrical.
The trophozoite has two nuclei, and large ventral sucking disc, and it moves
with a characteristic” falling leaf “ motion.
It is generally believed that the species is pathogenic, and clinicians agree
that it may be responsible for certain conditions such as diarrhea or
steatorrhea or symptoms referable to the gall bladder or duodenum.
The ovoidal cysts are distinctive in appearance and rather easily recognized.
They tend to be shed from the body in “showers” and negative stool
specimens may alternate with positive ones.
In general, multiplication of the flagellates occurs through longitudinal
binary fission of the trophozoites within the host. Giardia can multiply in the
cyst stage also – usually two organisms being produced upon excystation.
It has been suggested that Giardia may reproduce chiefly in the cyst stage,
encysting and excysting within the same host. However, binary fission of the
free trophozoites has been observed, and it is possible that multiplication
within the intestine may occur in both stages.

Symptoms:
Symptoms of this highly contagious disease include diarrhea, abdominal
pain, the passing of blood and mucus, hypoproteinemia with
hypogammaglobulinemia, fat soluble vitamin deficiencies, and the
production of copious light – colored fatty stools.
Early symptoms include flatulence, abdominal distension, nausea, and foul-
smelling bulky, often watery diarrhea. The stool contains excessive lipids
(steatorrhea) but very rarely any blood or necrotic tissue. Heavy infection
may lead to malabsorption of nutrients. The chronic stage is associated with
vitamin B12 malabsorption and lactose intolerance. Diagnosis
Symptoms, history, epidemiology are useful in diagnosis.
Dysentery caused by Giardia lamblia is distinct from other dysenteries due to
lack of mucus and blood in the stool and lack of light fever.
Cysts in the stool and trophozoites in the duodenal or jejunal aspirate (can be
recovered by the string test or enterotest capsule) can be identified
microscopically.
Trophozoite must be distinguished from the non- pathogenic flagellate
Trichomonas hominis.
Cysts may be excreted intermittently; therefore, it is important to examine
more than one stool sample.
Treatment
Metronidazole is the drug of choice

presence of an undulating membrane, or prominent cytostome are used more
often than nuclear detail for species identification.
Unlike the amebae, the shapes of both trophozoites and cysts of a given
species tend to be static and thus may serve for differentiation.
The trophozoite bodies are flexible but retain a characteristic shape, usually
elongate or “pear-shaped”.
All of the flagellates, are supplied with flagella, the fibrils that serve as
organs of locomotion, and each species is characterized by a definite number
and arrangement of these flagella and by a particular type of motion.
Simple direct life cycle, that involves no intermediate hosts.
Trichomonas species, occur only as trophozoites which therefore, serve as
both the diagnostic and the infective stage. No cyst stages are known in the
human species.
Trichomonas hominis and Trichomonas vaginalis are characterized by the
presence of an undulating membrane which, together with the flagella, give
the organism a peculiar ‘nervous, jerky’ type of motion.
Trichomonas tenax, found in the mouth, also parasitizes man. Its life cycle is
similar to those of the other two and transmission is through direct contact
(kissing).
In general, multiplication of the flagellates occurs through longitudinal
binary fission of the trophozoite within the host.
Trichomonas vaginalis:
Distribution: It is encountered in all climates and all social groups. It is a
cosmopolitan parasite (a worldwide distribution).
Habitat: Resides in the male and female urogenital tracts. In female it is
found mainly in vagina and in male it is in urethra.
The prevalence of this flagellate in developing countries is reported to be 5% - 20% in women and 2% -10% in men.
Morphology:
Important features – It exists only as a trophozoite form which have the
following characters: it is a pear- shaped organism measuring 10 to 30 micro
x 5 to 10 micro, it has short undulating membrane which comes up to the middle of the body, possesses 4 anterior flagella, a prominent axostyle which
bifurcates the body into two and projects posteriorly, there is a costa,
parabasal body, with rounded nucleus (anteriorly), chromatin granules are
present all over, more densely near costa and axostyle, flagella give
characteristic webbing or rotatory motility, and it multiplies by binary fission
in longitudinal axis.
Mode of transmission
Trichomonas vaginalis, a urogenital parasite. It is primarily a venereal
disease; Sexual intercourse is the primary mode of transmission.
Transmission can also be from person- to – person contact; either by contact
with contaminated articles; fomites (such as utensils, and furniture; toilet
articles, clothing), although this transmission is limited by liability of the
trophozoite. Rarely infants may be infected by passage through the mother’s
infected birth canal.
Incubation period: it varies from 4 to 30 days.
Pathogenesis and pathology
The trophozoite is found in the urethra and vagina of women and the urethra
and prostate gland of men. Within a few days following the introduction of
viable Trichomonas vaginalis into the vagina the proliferating colonies of
these flagellates cause degeneration and desquamation of the vaginal
epithelium. It is followed by leukocytic inflammation of the tissue layer. Very
large numbers of trichomonads and leukocytes are now present in the vaginal
secretion, which is liquid greenish, or yellow, and covers the mucous down to
the urethral orifice, vestibular glands, and clitoris.
As the acute condition changes to the chronic stage , the secretion loses its
purulent appearance due to a decrease in the number of trichomonads and
leukocytes, Trichomonas vaginalis in male genitalia may be symptomless or
may be responsible for an irritating, persistent, or recurring urethritis.
Clinical signs
Although the vagina is most commonly infected, trichomonas may spread to
all parts of the urogenital tract of the female.
The onset of symptoms such as vaginal or vulval pruritus and discharge is
often sudden and occurs during or after menstruation as a result of the
increased vaginal acidity.
The vaginal secretions are liquors, greenish or yellowish, sometimes frothy
and foul smelling. A frothy, creamy discharge is frequently observed in
infected females.
The disease may be complicated by concurrent fungal, bacterial, or
spirochetal infections.
Most infected women at the acute stage are asymptomatic or have a scanty,
watery vaginal discharge. In symptomatic cases vaginitis occurs with more
extensive inflammation, along with erosion of epithelial lining, and painful
urination, and results in symptomatic vaginal discharge, vulvitis and dysuria.
The vaginal secretion is extremely irritating, almost unbearable and is
constantly flowing. The symptoms may continue from a few days to many
months. After each menstruation there is a tendency for acute stage to recur.
The chronic condition transforms into latent one and secretions become
normal with no manifestation although trichomonas are still present.
Difference in the intensity of symptoms may be due to differences in
virulence of strains of this organism.
In males, infection is frequently asymptomatic (latent), or may be present as self- limited, persistent, or recurring urethritis, or may cause urethritis and
prostatis but severe symptoms involve not only the urethra (urethritis) and
bladder, but also the genital organs and glands, including the prostate.
Immunity
The infection may induce humoral, secretory, and cellular immune reactions,
but they are of little diagnostic help and do not appear to produce clinically
significant immunity.
Laboratory diagnosis
In female patient, Trichomonas vaginalis may be demonstrated in urine
sediment, wet preparations of vaginal secretions, or from vaginal scraping.
In male patient Trichomonas vaginalis may be found in the centrifuged urine
and wet preparations of prostatic secretions, or following massage of the
prostate glands
However, care should be taken to prevent contamination of the specimen
with feces, since Trichomonas hominis may be has seen and this misdiagnose
as Trichomonas vaginalis. The smear is stained using Giemsa, PAS,
Papanicolaou, Leishman, DIFF Quick and acridine orange

Treatment
Trichomoniasis is usually treated quickly and easily with antibiotics. Most
people are prescribed an antibiotic called metronidazole which is very
effective if taken correctly. You ‘ll usually have to take metronidazole twice a
day, for 5 to 7 days.
Sometimes this antibiotic can be prescribed in a single, large dose
Metronidazole is the drug of choice. If resistant cases occur, re- treatment
with higher doses is required.
Prevention
Both male and female sex partners must be treated to avoid reinfection
Good personal hygiene, avoidance of shared toilet articles and clothing
Safe sexual practice

Laboratory Diagnosis of enteric Protozoa
The routine methods used in laboratory diagnosis
Parasitic diseases may be presented by a wide variety of clinical
manifestations, depending on the invade tissue, or part in the host body.
Diagnosis of parasitic diseases depends on several laboratory methods, such
as: Direct microscopy; Imaging techniques; Endoscopy; and many other
developed techniques in addition to the clinical picture and geographical
location.
Diagnosis using direct microscopy is based on detecting the parasite by the
examination of different specimens such as the stool, urine, blood, CSF and
tissue biopsies.
Whereas immunodiagnostic techniques are depending on antigen and
antibody – detection assays.
In addition, molecular – based diagnostic approaches offer a great
sensitivity and specificity, and recently, the nanotechnology can also be
applied as diagnostic procedures utilizing nanodevices.
Proteo Stool specimens –specimen processing
Stool specimens can be examined fresh or preserved.
Examination of fresh specimens permits the observation of motile
trophozoites, but this must be carried out without delay.
Liquid (diarrheic) specimens (which are more likely to contain
trophozoites) should be examined within 30 minutes of passage) not within
30 minutes of arrival in the laboratory),
Soft specimens (which may contain both trophozoites and cysts) should be
examined within one hour of passage.
If delays cannot be avoided, the specimen should be preserved to avoid
disintegration of the trophozoites.
Formed specimens (less likely to contain trophozoites) can be kept for up
to one day, with overnight refrigeration if needed, prior to examination.

Microscopy
The examination for parasites by microscopy in routine diagnostic
laboratories is mostly requested on fecal samples.
Other specimens received for detection of parasites include, urine, sputum,
liver aspirates, duodenal aspirates, bile, corneal scrapings, contact lens
fluid, and tissue.
Diagnosis via microscopy requires nothing more than the microscope itself,
the specimens, and, for some techniques, a readily available dye.
Microscopy has been the mainstay diagnostic tool for decades, and even
though other techniques may exist, often results still require confirmation by
microscopy, since the newer diagnostic methods often lack
standardization.
Microscopy has several limitations in the diagnosis of parasitic infection.
Microscopy can be used for the diagnosis of infection with intestinal
protozoans, but the process is slow, very labor intensive, time –
consuming, and requires a high level of technical expertise. It also lacks
both sensitivity and specificity.
These procedures involve collecting several stool samples from patients on
different days and identifying the parasite within the sample.
Fecal collection over several days increases the sensitivity of microscopic
examination.
The popularity of the technique lies in its simplicity.

Diagnosis by protozoological methods
Protozoological methods of diagnosis fall into two broad groupings:
One suitable for those organisms that inhabit tissues, including blood;
Other for those, primarily inhabit the mucous surfaces of hollow viscera
communicating with the exterior.
Diagnosis of tissue protozoa can be done by the direct examination of an
easily accessible body fluids ; blood, CSF, lymph- node or tissue aspirates
and tissue biopsy.
The conspicuous motility of some of the stage of these protozoa is exploited
in wet mount microscopy.
1-Direct methods:
In which the organism is sought directly in specimens of tissue, or in the
contents of a hollow organs, are often helpful.
Direct examination methods are thus sometimes only of low efficiency. For
instance, in the direct examination of a wet mount of blood for trypanosomes,
which being motile are quickly recognized if seen.
Immunoassay systems
One of the body's defenses against parasites is its immunological system. On,
or in parasites, and in their secretory or excretory products, are many
proteins (antigens).
Against some of these (antigens), the body is capable of generating other
complex protein molecules (antibodies) that neutralize the antigens by
binding to them.
Antibodies are blood proteins produced in response to a specific antigen.

Nucleic Acid Amplification Tests (NAAT) for Enteric Parasites
Recently developed nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs), are automated
and rapid and show superior accuracy.
The most promising alternative platform for the detection of pathogenic
parasites in stool specimens is the nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT),
with polymerase chain reaction (PCR) being the best described.
NAATs traditional stool collection in formalin or other common preservatives
degrades the performance of NAATs, so, ideal specimens need to be provided
fresh or frozen.
Earlier studies focused on protozoa, where PCR typically was more sensitive
and specific compared to microscopy and also superior to antigen detection
assays and serology.
NAATs can also differentiate morphologically identical organisms, such as
Entamoeba histolytica and Entamoeba dispar.
