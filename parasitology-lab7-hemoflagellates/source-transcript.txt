General characteristics
Blood and tissue flagellates belong to the family
Trypanosomatidae.
The main genera pathogenic to humans are
Leishmania and Trypanosoma.
They inhabit mammalian blood or tissues and
develop in insect vectors.
Medical Parasitology · Lab 7
02
Basic cell structure
Each parasite has one nucleus, a kinetoplast,
and a flagellar apparatus.
The nucleus is usually round or oval and lies near
the centre.
The kinetoplast is a DNA-containing region of the
mitochondrion.
Medical Parasitology · Lab 7
03
Flagellum and axoneme
The flagellum is a thin, hair-like structure arising
from the basal body (blepharoplast).
Its internal supporting structure is the axoneme.
The flagellum may emerge freely or run along an
undulating membrane.

Staining characteristics
Giemsa and Leishman stains reveal internal
structures.
Cytoplasm: pale blue.
Nucleus and flagellum: pink to red-purple.
Kinetoplast: deep red-purple.
Medical Parasitology · Lab 7
07
Life cycle and multiplication
Medically important Leishmania and Trypanosoma
alternate between a mammalian host and an insect
vector.
Multiplication occurs mainly by binary fission.
Genetic exchange has also been demonstrated in
trypanosomatids.
Medical Parasitology · Lab 7
08
Leishmania: cutaneous disease
Cutaneous leishmaniasis is also called Oriental
sore.
Skin lesions develop at or near the sandfly bite
site.
Traditional species groupings include the L.
tropica and L. mexicana complexes.
Medical Parasitology · Lab 7
09
Leishmania: mucosal and visceral disease
Mucocutaneous disease (espundia): mainly
involves oral and nasal mucosa, sometimes after a
skin lesion.
The L. braziliensis complex is a classical cause.
Visceral disease (kala-azar): the L. donovani
complex affects the spleen, liver and bone marrow.

Amastigote: size and location
Rounded or oval, approximately
2–4 μm.
Usually lies inside
macrophages.
It may also occur in monocytes
and, less often, neutrophils.

Medical Parasitology · Lab 7
12
Amastigote: microscopic features
Pale blue cytoplasm and a large
red-purple nucleus.
A small, deeply stained
kinetoplast lies near the
nucleus.
There is no externally visible
flagellum.
Amastigotes in a touch preparation ·
CDC DPDx
Medical Parasitology · Lab 7
13
Promastigote: body and flagellum
Initially short, oval or pear-shaped.
Mature forms become elongated and
spindle-shaped.
Body: 15–25 μm long and 1.5–3.5 μm wide.
A single delicate flagellum measures about 15–28
μm.
Medical Parasitology · Lab 7
14
Promastigote: microscopic features
Giemsa or Leishman stain
reveals pale blue cytoplasm, a
pink nucleus and a red-purple
kinetoplast.
A single free anterior flagellum is
present, without an undulating
membrane.

Leishmania: hosts and infective form
The life cycle involves two hosts.
Mammalian hosts include humans and dogs.
The vector is the female sandfly.
The human-infective stage is the metacyclic
promastigote.

18
Leishmania transmission
Main route: bite of an infected female sandfly.
Less common routes include transmission from
mother to fetus and blood transfusion.
Accidental laboratory inoculation can also transmit
infection.
Medical Parasitology · Lab 7
19
Leishmania microscopy: specimens
For suspected visceral disease, specimens may
include:
Peripheral blood or bone marrow aspirate.
Splenic aspirate or an enlarged lymph node
aspirate.
Medical Parasitology · Lab 7
20
Leishmania microscopy: examination
Stain smears with Giemsa or Leishman stain.
Examine with the 100× oil objective (about 1000×
total magnification).
Look for amastigotes within macrophages. A few
extracellular forms may also appear.

Leishmania in peripheral blood
Amastigotes may occur in
monocytes and, less often,
neutrophils.
Scanty parasites can make
direct blood smears negative.
A thick blood film may improve
detection.

23
Leishmania culture
Inoculate blood or tissue material onto NNN
medium.
Incubate at 22–24°C for 1–4 weeks, according to
the laboratory protocol.
Each week, examine a drop of culture fluid for
promastigotes under high power.
Medical Parasitology · Lab 7
24
Immunological and molecular diagnosis
Antibody detection: supports the diagnosis in the
appropriate clinical setting.
Antigen detection: provides additional evidence
when a validated assay is available.
PCR: detects parasite DNA and can assist species
identification.
Medical Parasitology · Lab 7
25
Leishmanin skin test: method
Inject 0.1 mL of killed promastigote suspension
intradermally into the forearm.
Read the reaction after 48–72 hours.
An induration of at least 5 mm is conventionally
positive.
Medical Parasitology · Lab 7
26
Leishmanin skin test: interpretation
A positive reaction indicates previous exposure
and cellular immune sensitisation.
The test is usually negative in active kala-azar.
After cure, it may become positive around 6–8
weeks, although timing varies.
Medical Parasitology · Lab 7
27
Blood picture and liver tests
Progressive leukopenia may occur in visceral
leishmaniasis.
The white blood cell count may fall to 1000/mm³ or
lower.
Relative increases in lymphocytes and monocytes
may occur.
Liver function tests may show mild elevations.
Medical Parasitology · Lab 7
28
Trypanosoma: human diseases
African trypanosomiasis (sleeping sickness) is
caused by T. brucei gambiense and T. brucei
rhodesiense.
American trypanosomiasis (Chagas disease) is
caused by T. cruzi.
Medical Parasitology · Lab 7
29
African trypanosomes: habitat
The parasites first involve blood and regional
lymph nodes.
They may later invade the central nervous
system.
Both T. b. gambiense and T. b. rhodesiense occur
as trypomastigotes in humans.
Medical Parasitology · Lab 7
30
African trypanosomes: hosts
The life cycle involves a mammalian host and the
tsetse fly.
Humans are the main reservoir for T. b.
gambiense.
Wild and domestic animals are important reservoirs
for T. b. rhodesiense.
Medical Parasitology · Lab 7
31
African trypanosomes: blood forms
Bloodstream trypomastigotes
are pleomorphic.
Long slender, short broad
(stumpy) and intermediate
forms occur.
Stumpy forms have a short or
inconspicuous free flagellum.

Medical Parasitology · Lab 7
32
African trypanosomes: vector forms
In the fly gut, procyclic trypomastigotes develop
and multiply.
Epimastigotes occur during development in the
salivary glands.
Metacyclic trypomastigotes are the
human-infective forms.

34
African trypanosome transmission
The tsetse fly bite transmits infective metacyclic
trypomastigotes.
The parasite enters the host with the fly’s saliva.
Congenital transmission has also been recorded.
Medical Parasitology · Lab 7
35
African trypanosomiasis: diagnosis
Demonstrate trypanosomes in blood, lymph node
aspirate or bone marrow.
CSF examination helps assess central nervous
system involvement.
Examine appropriately prepared Giemsa-stained
samples.
Medical Parasitology · Lab 7
36
Low parasitaemia: concentration methods
Low parasite numbers can make
direct microscopy negative.
Concentrating blood before
microscopy improves the
chance of detecting
trypanosomes.

37
Trypanosoma cruzi
T. cruzi causes Chagas disease, also called
American or South American trypanosomiasis.
In humans, it occurs as amastigotes in tissues
and trypomastigotes in peripheral blood.
Medical Parasitology · Lab 7
38
T. cruzi: tissue amastigotes
Amastigotes occur within cells,
including muscular and nervous
tissues.
They resemble Leishmania
amastigotes morphologically.
This is the multiplying stage in
humans.

39
T. cruzi: bloodstream trypomastigotes
These forms circulate in humans
and other mammalian hosts.
They do not multiply in the
bloodstream.
A blood-feeding triatomine bug
ingests these parasites.

Medical Parasitology · Lab 7
40
T. cruzi: trypomastigote morphology
Stained blood forms may curve into C, U or S
shapes.
The posterior end may appear pointed or
wedge-shaped.
The free flagellum may measure about one-third
of the body length.
Medical Parasitology · Lab 7
41
T. cruzi: epimastigotes
Epimastigotes develop in the
midgut of the triatomine bug.
The kinetoplast lies adjacent to,
and anterior to, the nucleus.
A short undulating membrane
runs along the anterior part of
the body.
T. cruzi epimastigote from culture ·
CDC DPDx
Medical Parasitology · Lab 7
42
T. cruzi: development in the bug
Epimastigotes multiply by binary fission in the
midgut.
They differentiate into metacyclic
trypomastigotes in the hindgut.
The infective forms leave the bug in its faeces.

T. cruzi: hosts and vector
The life cycle involves humans
or other mammals and a
triatomine (reduviid) bug.
These vectors are commonly
called kissing bugs.
Metacyclic trypomastigotes in
bug faeces infect humans.

T. cruzi: vector transmission
The bug defecates during or after its blood meal.
Faeces containing metacyclic trypomastigotes
contaminate a bite wound, damaged skin or a
mucous membrane.
Rubbing or scratching can help the parasite enter.
