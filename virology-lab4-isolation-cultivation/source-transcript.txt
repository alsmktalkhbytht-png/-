Isolation and Cultivation of Viruses
Introduction
Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites, meaning they require
living host cells to replicate. To study viruses, it is essential to
isolate and cultivate them in the laboratory. This involves
providing them with suitable host cells and conditions for
replication.

Methods of Viral Cultivation
1. Cell Culture: The most common method, involving growing
viruses in cultured cells derived from various animal tissues.
2. Primary cell cultures: Derived directly from tissues, have limited
lifespan.
3. Continuous cell lines: Immortalized (not dying) cells that can be
propagated indefinitely.
4. Embryonated Eggs: Used for cultivating some viruses, such as
influenza and herpes simplex viruses.
5. Animal Inoculation: Used for viruses that cannot be grown in
cell culture or eggs, such as rabies and poliovirus.
Steps in Viral Isolation and Cultivation
1. Sample Collection: Obtain clinical specimens (e.g., blood,
respiratory secretions, stool) from infected individuals.
1

2. Sample Preparation: Inactivate or remove interfering substances,
such as antibiotics or bacteria.
3. Inoculation: Introduce the prepared sample into the chosen host
system (cell culture, embryonated eggs, or animals).
4. Incubation: Provide appropriate conditions for viral replication
(temperature, humidity, etc.).
5. Observation: Monitor for signs of viral infection, such as
cytopathic effects (CPE) in cell cultures or disease symptoms in
animals.
6. Virus Identification: Use various techniques to identify the
isolated virus, such as:
-- Electron microscopy: Visualize viral particles directly.
-- Serological tests: Detect virus-specific antibodies.
-- Nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs): Amplify and detect
viral DNA or RNA.

Isolation of Viruses
1. Viruses cannot be grown on an artificial nutrient medium,
viruses require a living host cell for replication.
2. Infected host cells (eukaryotic or prokaryotic) can be cultured
and grown, and then the growth medium can be harvested as a
source of virus.
3. Virions (virus particles) in the liquid medium can be separated
from the host cells by either centrifugation or filtration.
4. Filters can physically remove anything present in the solution
that is larger than the virions; the viruses can then be collected in
the filtrate (Figure 1).

Cultivation of Viruses
1. Viruses can be grown in vivo (within a whole living organism,
plant, or animal) or in vitro (outside a living organism in cells in an
artificial environment, such as a test tube, cell culture flask, or agar
plate).
2. Bacteriophages can be grown in the presence of a dense layer of
bacteria (also called a bacterial lawn) grown in a 0.7 % soft agar in
a Petri dish or flat (horizontal) flask (Figure 2 a). The agar
3

concentration is decreased from the 1.5% usually used in culturing
bacteria. The soft 0.7% agar allows the bacteriophages to easily
diffuse through the medium.
For lytic bacteriophages, lysing of the bacterial hosts can then be
readily observed when a clear zone called a plaque is detected
(Figure 2b). As the phage kills the bacteria, many plaques are
observed among the cloudy bacterial lawn.

Figure 2. (a) Flasks like this may be used to culture human or animal
cells for viral culturing. (b) These plates contain bacteriophage T4
grown on an Escherichia coli lawn. Clear plaques are visible where host
bacterial cells have been lysed. Viral titers increase on the plates to the
left.

3. Animal viruses require cells within a host animal or tissue-culture cells derived from an animal.
Animal virus cultivation is important for:
1) identification and diagnosis of pathogenic viruses in clinical
specimens.
2) production of vaccines.
3) basic research studies.
A. In vivo host sources can be a developing embryo in an
embryonated bird’s egg (e.g., chicken, turkey) or a whole animal.
4

For example, most of the influenza vaccine manufactured for
annual flu vaccination programs is cultured in hens’ eggs.
The embryo or host animal serves as an incubator for viral
replication (Figure 3).

Figure 3. (a) The cells within chicken eggs are used to culture different
types of viruses. (b) Viruses can be replicated in various locations within
the egg, including the chorioallantoic membrane, the amniotic cavity,
and the yolk sac.

B. For in vitro studies, various types of cells can be used to support
the growth of viruses. A primary cell culture is freshly prepared
from animal organs or tissues. Cells are extracted from tissues by
mechanical scraping or mincing to release cells or by an enzymatic
method using trypsin or Collagenase to break up tissue and release
single cells into suspension. In contrast to primary cell cultures,
continuous cell lines, usually derived from transformed cells or
tumors, are often able to be subcultured many times or even grown
indefinitely (in which case they are called immortal). (Figure 4).

Figure 4. Cells for culture are prepared by separating them from their
tissue matrix. (a) Primary cell cultures grow attached to the surface of

once they become too dense and begin touching each other. At this point,

Continuous cell cultures are not affected by contact inhibition. They
continue to grow regardless of cell density.

Detection of a Virus
Regardless of the method of cultivation, once a virus has been
introduced into a whole host organism, embryo, or tissue-culture
cell, a sample can be prepared from the infected host, embryo, or
cell line for further analysis under a brightfield, electron, or
fluorescent microscope.
Cytopathic effects (CPEs) are distinct observable cell abnormalities
due to viral infection. CPEs can include loss of adherence to the
surface of the container, changes in cell shape from flat to round,
shrinkage of the nucleus, vacuoles in the cytoplasm, fusion of
cytoplasmic membranes and the formation of multinucleated
syncytia, inclusion bodies in the nucleus or cytoplasm, and complete
cell lysis (Figure 5).
