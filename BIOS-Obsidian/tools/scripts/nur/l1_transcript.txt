Statistical terms:
• Birth rate: The number of live births per 1000 population per year.
• Infant mortality rate: The no. of deaths per 1000 live births occurring on the first 12 months of extra uterine life.
Factors affect the infant mortality rate:
 Birth weight. / Gestational age. / Maternal age. / Low level of maternal education.
Why information on mortality and morbidity is important for nurses?
• Health during childhood:
  o Health is traditionally assessed by observing mortality "death" and morbidity "illness".
  o Information concerning mortality and morbidity is important for nurses because this knowledge provides a rationale for planning and delivering care.
  o Such data provide information about: the causes of death and illness / high - risk age groups for disorders or hazards / advances in treatment and prevention / specific areas of health counseling

• Infant Morbidity:
  o Infant morbidity: prevalence of a specific illness in a population at a particular time — Acute illness / Chronic disease / Disability
• Acute Illness Defined:
  o "disease with an abrupt onset and usually a short course"
  o "illness of short duration, rapidly progressive, and in need of urgent care"
• Acute Illness:
  o Defining characteristics: Starts suddenly and are short lived / "Runs its course" regardless of drug intervention
• Chronic Illness Defined:
  o "illnesses that are prolonged, do not resolve spontaneously, and are rarely cured completely"
  o "a disease or disorder developing slowly and persisting for a long period of time, often for the remainder of the lifetime of the individual"
Introduction:
 Nurse is one of the important components of the health team in caring for children.
 The pediatric nurse given direct bedside care to sick children, guide families in their care for their children during health and illness, in hospitals and at homes.
Pediatric Nursing:
• It is the art and science of giving nursing care to children from birth through adolescent with emphasis on the physical growth, mental, emotional, and psychosocial development.
Scope of pediatric nursing practices
• Nurses have the responsibility in providing nursing interventions either in ambulatory or institution setting.
• Ambulatory setting such as home, school, and or physician's clinic where children and their parents have health or counseling needs.
[p3]
• While institutional settings are mainly hospitals (general or specialized hospitals) where care is provided to sick children and their parents.
Role of a Pediatric Nurse
 o Direct / Educator / Counselor / Consultant / Advocate / Care Coordinator, or health systems manager / Researcher
The good pediatric nurse must be (Qualified):
 1. Good observer. 2. Honest and truthful 3. Sympathetic, kind, patient and cheerful 4. Love to work with children. 5. Interested in family care 6. Able to provide teaching to children and their families
The goals of nursing care of children
Based on primary health care are :
 1. Promote the healthy maturation of the child as a physical, intellectual, and emotional being within the context of his family and communities (primary level)
 2. Provide health care for the child who requires treatment from diseases (secondary Level).
 3. Dealing with the child's disabilities (tertiary level).
The Role of the Pediatric Nursing includes:
In primary level :
 Though health education to child and his parents and providing child's basic needs and immunization. It can:
 a) Maintain child's health . b) Help the child achieves his optimal growth and development. c) Prevent diseases and their complications.
In secondary level :
 a) Assessing their needs . b) Nursing diagnosis .
[p4]
 c) Planning for their care . d) Implementing the plan . e) Evaluation children's condition . f) Providing health teaching to children and their parents.
In tertiary level :
 a) Assist children to return to their maximal level of functioning following illness and/ or disabilities.

Family centered care :
 It based on philosophy that quality care can be provided in an environment that support family integrity and promote psychological and physiological health of the family
 • Health team should be flexible and creative in caring for the child within his family as their intervention are based on the needs of parent and child and on their coping resources.
 • Although the family is the ultimate coordinator of its own care, the nurse can help establish a positive environment for family members and help them accept and utilize the care provided. He\She can guide family members through the unknown of new experiences in seeking holistic health.

Communication skills (With Children and Their Families)
Communication : is a two-way process by which information exchanged between individuals with use of language, behaviors, or symbols.
Or it's a complex process that including the perception and judgments of all individuals involved.
 Communication may be, verbal and nonverbal.
Verbal communication: may involve language and its expression: vocalization in the form of laughs, moans, and squalls
Nonverbal communication: often called "body language" includes gestures, movements, facial expressions, postures, and reactions.
Communication according developmental stage
Infancy: Because they are unable to use words, primarily use and understand nonverbal communication.
Early Childhood: Children younger than 5 years old are egocentric. They see things only in relation to themselves and from their point of view. Although they have not yet acquired sufficient language skills to express their feelings and wants, toddlers can effectively use their hands to communicate ideas without words.
School-Age Years: They want explanations and reasons for everything but require no verification beyond that.

Communicating with Children
Effective listening is the key to successful communication.
Allow children time to feel comfortable.
• Avoid sudden or rapid advances, broad smiles, extended eye contact, or other gestures. • Talk to the parent if child is initially shy. • Communicate through transition objects such as dolls, and stuffed animals. • Give older children the opportunity to talk without the parents present. • Assume a position that is at eye level with child. • Speak in a quiet, unhurried, and confident voice. • Speak clearly, be specific, and use simple words and short sentences. • State directions and suggestions positively. • Offer a choice only when one exists. • Be honest with children. • Allow them to express their concerns and fears. • Use a variety of communication techniques

Immunity and immunization for children
• Infants and children are susceptible to infectious diseases because their immune systems are immature. As children mature and grow, their exposure to infectious agents increases and they develop antibodies naturally.
• Immunization: The process of inducing or providing active or passive immunity artificially by administering an immunobiologic.
• Immunity: is the ability of the body to protect against all types of foreign bodies that enter the body. Immunity also called disease resistance.
• Immune protection: Children receive protection from infectious diseases naturally and artificially.
[p9]
Immunization is the key disease prevention. The development of effective vaccines, beginning in the 1940s, revolutionized children's health care . Immunization shift care from disease treatment to disease prevention .
The nurse needs to understand the principles of immunizations, the proper use of vaccines, and barriers to immunization.
Methods of obtaining immune protection:
Immune protection can obtained in five different methods:
 1. Natural (innate) immunity: Innate immunity is a combination of natural and nonspecific immunity that can protect the human body from pathogens and foreign agents (For example, the phagocytic action of white blood cells (macrophages)
 2. Naturally acquired active immunity: is obtained when the body's immune system responds to a specific pathogen. Antibodies and memory cells prevent or reduce the severity of infection with that specific pathogen. It persists for many years.
 3. Naturally acquired passive immunity: involves mother-to-fetus transmission of maternal antibodies (immunoglobulin G crosses the placenta). After birth, the infant can receive passive immunity through maternal antibodies in breast milk. It lasts only as long as the antibodies remain in the blood (usually from a few weeks to about 2 months). Some antibodies remain up to age 1 year, such measles.
 4. Artificially acquired active immunity: is achieved by administration of a vaccine or toxoid. The vaccine or toxoid stimulates the immune system's production of antibodies against a specific antigen, but symptoms of the disease aren't produced.
 5. Artificially acquired passive immunity: administration of a specific immunoglobulin (when antibodies developed in another person or animal donor are injected into an individual usually intravenous)
 [figure] Types of Immunity: ACTIVE IMMUNITY (Natural: Infection; Artificial: Vaccination) — PASSIVE IMMUNITY (Natural: Maternal antibodies; Artificial: Monoclonal antibodies)
Terms:
Immunity: An inherited or acquired state, in which the individual resistant to the infectious agent
Antibody: A protein, found mostly in serum, formed in response to antigen exposure
Antigen: A variety of foreign substances, including bacteria, viruses, toxins, and foreign proteins, that stimulate the formation of antibodies.
[p10]
Immunization: process of inducing (active or passive immunity) artificially (response of vaccination)
Vaccination: administer of vaccine to individual
Vaccine: A suspension of (attenuated or inactivated microorganisms or fractions of microorganism) administered to stimulate immunity
Attenuate: Reduce the virulence of microorganism by heat or chemicals
Immunobiologic: antigenic substance (e.g., vaccines and toxoids) or antibody containing preparations (e.g., globulins and antitoxins) from human or animal donors, used for active or passive immunization or therapy.
Toxoids: a modified bacterial toxin that has been made nontoxic but retains the ability to stimulate the formation of antitoxin.
Antitoxin: a solution of antibodies (e.g., diphtheria antitoxin and botulinum antitoxin) derived from the serum of animals immunized with specific antigens and used to confer passive immunity and for treatment.
Immune globulin (IG): or intravenous immune globulin (IVIG): a sterile solution containing antibodies from large pools of human blood plasma; primarily indicated for routine maintenance of immunity of certain immunodeficient persons and for passive immunization against measles and hepatitis A.
Specific immune globulins: special preparations obtained from blood plasma from donor pools preselected for a high antibody content against a specific antigen (e.g., hepatitis B immune globulin, varicella zoster immune globulin, rabies immune globulin, tetanus immune globulin, vaccinia immune globulin, and cytomegalovirus immune globulin); as with IG and IVIG, do not transmit hepatitis B virus, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) or other infectious disease.
There are a few different types of vaccines. They are including:
 Attenuated (weakened) live viruses are used in some vaccines such as in the measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine.
 Killed (inactivated) viruses or bacteria are used in some vaccines, such as in IPV.
 Toxoid vaccines contain an inactivated toxin produced by the bacterium. For example, the diphtheria and tetanus vaccines.
 Conjugate vaccines (such as Haemophilus influenza type B HIB) contain parts of bacteria combined with proteins.
Indication of Immunization
 1. Immunizations protect you or your child from dangerous diseases.
[p11]
 2. Immunizations help reduce the spread of disease to others. 3. Vaccines protect not only yourself but also others around you 4. Getting immunized costs less than getting treated for the diseases that the shots protect you from.

Contraindications to vaccine administration
 1. Mild illnesses and low-grade fevers that are common in children are not contraindications . 2. However, there are several reasons to withhold or delay vaccine administration : 3. Child with moderate to severe illness or a history of allergic response or anaphylaxis to vaccine . 4. Vaccination with containing live or attenuated viruses not given to child with immunodeficiency disease (leukemic), or receiving immunosuppressive therapy . 5. The DTAP vaccine not given to a child who has a progressive and active central nervous system (CNS) problem. However, a child with cerebral palsy can receive immunizations . 6. Feverish child 7. past history of intussusceptions
