                                                                                     Growth hormone

Growth hormone (GH), peptide hormone comprising 191 amino acids synthesized and
secreted by the anterior lobe of the pituitary gland by cells called somatotrophs. It
stimulates the growth of essentially all tissues of the body, including bone.

Disorders Associated with Growth Hormone
       Excess GH:
                      o Gigantism (in children): Abnormal growth due to excess GH before
                      epiphyseal plate closure.
                      o Acromegaly (in adults): Enlargement of hands, feet, and facial features due
                      to GH excess after growth plates have closed.
                      o Causes: Typically, due to pituitary adenomas.




             Deficiency of GH:

                      o In children, it causes growth retardation or dwarfism.

Diagnostic Approaches

       • GH Stimulation Tests:

                      o Used to assess GH deficiency (e.g., insulin tolerance test).

       • GH Suppression Tests:

                      o Used to diagnose GH excess (e.g., oral glucose tolerance test).

       • Measurement of IGF-1 Levels:

                      o Reflects average GH activity.

Common GH Stimulation Tests:

1-Insulin Tolerance Test (ITT):

                              Purpose: Evaluates the pituitary gland's GH release in response to
                              hypoglycemia.
                              Procedure:

                                     Patient fasts overnight. Baseline blood samples are taken to measure
                              glucose and GH levels. Insulin is administered intravenously to induce
                              hypoglycemia (target blood glucose < 40 mg/dL). Blood samples are
                              collected at intervals (0, 30, 60, 90and 120 minutes). GH levels are measured
                              in the samples.
                             Interpretation: A peak GH level > 10 ng/mL in children or > 5 ng/mL in
                              adults is normal.

Diagnosis of GH excess
•The diagnosis of GH excess is suggested by the clinical presentation, biochemical tests
and radiological findings of the pituitary.
•CT or MRI body scanning may help to find an ectopic source of GH or GHRH.
•(MRI) is more sensitive than (CT) scanning.
•Diagnosis requires the measurement of GH and IGF‐1.
•Random basal plasma GH is often very high in these patients, but the concentrations are
too variable for accurate diagnosis and overlap with the normal reference range of GH,.
•Random GH measurements are often not diagnostic owing to episodic secretion and a
short half-life.
•However, a random GH below 0.5 μg/L essentially excludes a diagnosis of acromegaly if
IGF‐1 is within the reference range, whereas an elevated IGF‐1 should prompt further
investigation with a glucose tolerance test .
•Reference ranges for IGF‐1 are age and method specific.
•The diagnosis is confirmed by demonstrating a raised plasma GH concentration that is
not suppressed by a rise in plasma glucose concentration. (Glucose suppression test ).

The response of plasma GH to a glucose suppression test (glucose tolerance test) is the
‘gold standard’ for the diagnosis of acromegaly.
Procedure
1.After an overnight fast, insert an indwelling intravenous cannula.
2.After at least 30 min, take basal samples for plasma glucose and GH estimation.
3.The patient should drink 75 g of glucose dissolved in about 300 mL of water, or an
equivalent glucose load.
4.Take samples for glucose and GH assays at 30, 60, 90 and 120 min after the glucose
load has been taken.
     Interpretation
       1.In normal subjects, plasma GH falls to less than 0.5 μg/L. However, in patients with
       acromegaly or gigantism, GH does not fall in response to the stimulus of
       hyperglycaemia, and may even increase in about 30% of patients.
