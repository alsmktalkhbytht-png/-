Why We Stain?
The full term is Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining.
It is the gold standard for histological examination, used to visualize tissue
structure under a microscope. Over 80% of all tissue diagnostics start with an H&E
stain.

A. Hematoxylin
A basic dye derived from the logwood tree (Haematoxylum campechianum).
What it stains: Acidic (acidophilic) structures.
Color: Stains them a bluish-purple or dark blue.

Targets:
Cell Nucleus (because DNA/RNA is acidic)
Rough Endoplasmic Reticulum
Cartilage

2. Eosin
What it stains: Basic (basophilic) structures.
Color: Stains them shades of pink, red, or orange.

Targets:
 Cytoplasm
 Muscle fibers
 Collagen
 Red blood cells
 Keratin

The Staining Process
1- Tissue Preparation: A thin slice of tissue (a "section") is mounted on a glass slide.

2- Hematoxylin: The slide is immersed in hematoxylin, which stains the
nuclei dark blue.

3- Rinsing: Excess dye is washed off.

4- "Bluing": The section is placed in a weak alkaline
solution (like ammonia water or Scott's tap water) to turn
the hematoxylin a permanent, crisp blue color.

B. Eosin:
The slide is then immersed in eosin, which stains the
cytoplasmic and extracellular components pink.

Dehydration & Mounting: The slide is dehydrated
through alcohols, cleared, and sealed with a coverslip
for preservation.

 The Final Result: A Diagnostic Picture
 Under the microscope, an H&E-stained slide provides a classic
and highly informative color contrast:
 Nuclei: Dark Blue/Purple

Cytoplasm & Connective Tissue: Pink

Muscle Fibers: Deeper Pink

Red Blood Cells: Bright Orange/Red
