Why We Stain?
Fresh, untreated tissue is mostly transparent and colorless when sliced thinly.
Under a microscope, it reveals very little detail because all cellular components
have similar refractive indices.

Staining : is the process of applying colored
dyes or metals to tissue sections to:
1- Impart Contrast: Make different structures visible.
2- Identify Tissue Components: Differentiate between nuclei, cytoplasm,
connective tissues, etc.
3- Reveal Chemical Composition: Indicate the presence of specific molecules
(e.g., carbohydrates, proteins).
4- Identify Microorganisms: Detect bacteria, fungi, and other pathogens.
5- Aid in Diagnosis: Provide the primary visual basis for pathological diagnosis.

How Dyes Bind to Tissues
The Chemical Basis of Staining:
The interaction between dyes and tissue components is primarily chemical, relying on
affinity. The two main theories are:

A. The Physical Theory
This involv es non-chemical, mechanical forces.
• Adsorption: The dye is physically attracted and
adheres to the surface of tissue components via weak
Van der Waals forces.

• Absorption: The dye is taken up by the tissue, much like
a sponge soaking up water, and becomes trapped within
its pores or matrix.
•

Solubility: Some dyes are more soluble in certain
tissue lipids than in the solvent, causing them to
precipitate out of solution and into the lipid (e.g.,
Sudan stains for fats).

B. The Chemical Theory
This is the most important and widely accepted theory. It proposes
that staining results from chemical reactions or bonds between the
dye and specific tissue components. The key players are:
1. Acid-Base Reactions (Ionic Bonding): This is the cornerstone of
routine staining.
 Basic Dyes: These are cations (positively charged). They have an affinity for
acidophilic (acid-loving) components, which are anionic (negatively
charged).
 Examples of Basic Dyes: Hematoxylin, Methylene Blue, Toluidine Blue.

 They Stain: Nucleic acids (DNA/RNA) in the nucleus, ribosomes, and
cartilage matrix. These structures are rich in phosphate and sulfate
groups, which carry a negative charge.

Acidic Dyes: These are anions (negatively charged). They have
an affinity for basophilic (base-loving) components, which are
cationic (positively charged).
Examples of Acidic Dyes: Eosin, Acid Fuchsin, Picric Acid.

They Stain: Most cytoplasmic proteins, collagen, and
red blood cells. These structures are rich in amino
groups, which carry a positive charge.
1- The Classic Example: Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E)
* Hematoxylin (basic) stains the nuclei
blue-purple (basophilic).
* Eosin (acidic) stains the cytoplasm
and connective tissue pink
(acidophilic).
* This provides a fundamental contrast
that allows pathologists to assess tissue
architecture.

 2. Other Chemical Mechanisms:
• Mordants: These are not dyes themselves but "linker"
substances that form a complex between the dye and the
tissue, creating a strong, insoluble "lake." The bond is much
more intense and permanent.
• Example: Hematoxylin is a weak dye on its own. When used
with an alum mordant (usually potassium alum), it forms
Hematein-Aluminum Lake, which is a powerful nuclear stain.
Iron is another common mordant.

Reduction: Some staining methods rely on the tissue component reducing a
metallic salt to a visible, colored precipitate.
Example: Silver impregnation techniques for reticular fibers (e.g., Gordon &
Sweet's stain). The tissue reduces the silver salt to metallic silver, which
deposits on the fibers, making them black.

Histochemical Reactions: These are specific chemical reactions
that produce a colored product in situ.
Example: Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) Stain. Periodic acid oxidizes
sugar groups in carbohydrates (like in basement membranes,
glycogen) to create aldehydes. These aldehydes then react with
Schiff's reagent to produce a magenta color.

Classification of Stains by Purpose
Stains can be categorized based on what they are
designed to demonstrate.
 A. Routine Stains
 • H&E (Hematoxylin and Eosin): The workhorse of histology and
pathology. Used for general tissue morphology.
 B. Special Stains
 These highlight specific structures that are not clearly visible with H&E.

 C- Connective Tissue Stains:
 Masson's Trichrome: Stains nuclei (black/dark blue), cytoplasm (red), and collagen
(blue/green).

Van Gieson's Stain: Stains collagen red and
muscle yellow.

Verhoeff-Van Gieson Stain: Highlights elastic
fibers (black/blue-black).

Carbohydrate Stains:
PAS (Periodic Acid-Schiff): Stains carbohydrates
(glycogen, glycoproteins, proteoglycans) magenta.

Mucicarmine: Specifically stains acid mucins (e.g.,
in adenocarcinomas) red.

Ziehl-Neelsen Stain: For acid-fast bacilli like Mycobacterium
tuberculosis (red rods).

Microorganism Stains:
Gram Stain: Differentiates Gram-positive (purple) and
Gram-negative (pink) bacteria.
