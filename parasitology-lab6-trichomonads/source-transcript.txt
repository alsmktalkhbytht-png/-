Introduction: What Are Trichomonads?

• Trichomonads are unicellular eukaryotic protozoa belonging to the group Parabasalia.

• They typically move using flagella; some possess an undulating membrane that assists motility.

• The recognized human-associated species are known primarily as trophozoites; no true cyst
stage is known for the main human species discussed here.

• Clinical relevance depends on the species and its site: the urogenital tract, oral cavity, or
intestine.

• The genus name Trichomonas has been used broadly in older literature, so current taxonomic
names should be checked.

[Slide 3]

Species Covered in This Presentation

• Trichomonas vaginalis: a sexually transmitted urogenital pathogen.

• Trichomonas tenax: primarily an oral trichomonad associated with poor oral health and
periodontal disease; its causal role is not fully established.

• Pentatrichomonas hominis (historically called Trichomonas hominis): an intestinal trichomonad
generally considered nonpathogenic or of uncertain clinical significance.

• Trichomonas gallinae: an important parasite of birds, included for taxonomic comparison rather
than as a routine human parasite.

• The main clinical focus is on the three species associated with humans.

[Slide 4]

General Characteristics

• Unicellular organisms; the trophozoite is the recognized form in the main human-associated
species.

• Reproduction occurs by longitudinal binary fission.

• Flagella are used for motility; the number of flagella and extent of the undulating membrane
vary by species.

• No resistant cyst stage is known for the main human species; transmission generally occurs
through direct contact or suitable moist secretions.

• Motility in wet preparations is affected by specimen freshness, transport conditions, and
temperature.

Trichomonas vaginalis


[Slide 6]

1. Trichomonas vaginalis — Introduction and Medical Importance

• T. vaginalis is the most clinically important human
trichomonad and causes trichomoniasis.

• It infects the urogenital tract: the vagina, cervix, and
urethra in women, and the urethra and sometimes
prostate in men.

• Transmission occurs mainly through sexual contact, and
infection may remain asymptomatic.

• The World Health Organization estimated approximately
156 million new infections worldwide in 2020 among
people aged 15–49 years.

• Trichomoniasis is treatable, but reinfection is common if
sexual partners are not treated.

[Slide 7]

T. vaginalis — Morphology and General Features

• The trophozoite is pear-shaped or oval and is
approximately 7–30 µm long.

• It has one anterior nucleus, four anterior flagella, and a fifth
flagellum associated with the undulating membrane.

• An axostyle extends toward the posterior end and may
project beyond it.

• Motility is rapid and jerky or tumbling; this can be a helpful
feature in a fresh wet mount.

• No true cyst stage is known, and the organism does not
survive well in the external environment.

T. vaginalis — Epidemiology and Risk Factors

• Infection occurs worldwide, with prevalence varying by population, age group, and diagnostic
method.

• Risk is increased by factors such as multiple sexual partners and other sexually transmitted
infections.

• Many infected women and men have no symptoms; absence of symptoms does not exclude
infection.

• Transmission through swimming pools or toilet seats is not considered the usual or
epidemiologically established route.

• Assessment for other sexually transmitted infections may be appropriate according to the
clinical context.

[Slide 10]

T. vaginalis — Pathogenesis and Clinical Features

• The parasite adheres to urogenital epithelial cells and can cause cellular injury and local
inflammation.

• In women, symptoms may include vaginal discharge, itching, erythema, dysuria, or pain during
intercourse; infection may also be asymptomatic.

• Vaginal pH may be above 4.5, but this finding is nonspecific and cannot establish the diagnosis.

• In men, infection is often asymptomatic; urethritis, discharge, dysuria, and occasionally
prostatitis may occur.

• Trichomoniasis is associated with increased risk of HIV acquisition and some adverse
reproductive outcomes; these associations do not mean every infection causes such outcomes.

[Slide 11]

T. vaginalis — Life Cycle

• The trophozoite is the recognized form in
humans; no cyst stage is known.

• The parasite is transmitted mainly during
sexual contact through infected genital
secretions.

• It colonizes the urogenital tract and multiplies
by longitudinal binary fission.

• Trophozoites may be present in genital
secretions or urine and can be passed to a
sexual partner during contact.

• Both the infective and diagnostic forms are
trophozoites; cysts are not expected in
specimens.

[Slide 12]

T. vaginalis — Laboratory Diagnosis: Specimens

• For women, a vaginal swab or vaginal discharge is a
preferred specimen; other specimens may be used according
to the validated test.

• For men, urine or a urethral swab may be used; specimen
choice depends on the test platform.

• Wet mount: a fresh specimen is mixed with saline and
examined promptly for motile trophozoites.

• Examination should be performed quickly because wet-
mount sensitivity decreases substantially as motility is lost.

• Collect and transport specimens according to laboratory
and assay instructions, while maintaining privacy and
informed consent.

[Slide 13]

T. vaginalis — Laboratory Diagnosis: Methods and Interpretation

• NAAT/PCR methods are among the most sensitive options and are preferred when a validated
test is available for the specimen type.

• Wet mount is rapid and inexpensive, but its sensitivity is limited; a negative result does not rule
out infection.

• Culture in a specialized medium may be used in selected situations, including persistent
infection or drug-susceptibility assessment.

• Rapid antigen tests are available for certain specimens and populations; follow the approved
indications of the specific test.

• A Pap smear is not a definitive diagnostic test. If organisms are incidentally observed,
confirmation with an appropriate sensitive test is recommended.

[Slide 14]

T. vaginalis — Prevention and Treatment Overview

• Effective treatment generally uses nitroimidazoles such as metronidazole or tinidazole,
according to clinical guidelines.

• Sexual partners should be treated, and sexual contact should be avoided until treatment is
completed and symptoms have resolved as advised.

• Retesting women at approximately 3 months is recommended because reinfection is common.

• Self-medication or unsupervised topical treatment is not recommended; regimen selection
belongs to a qualified healthcare professional.

• This slide is educational and is not an individualized prescription.

TRICHOMONAS TENAX

[Slide 16]

2. Trichomonas tenax — Introduction and Habitat

• T. tenax is a trichomonad found mainly in the oral cavity, especially dental
plaque and periodontal pockets.

• Its presence is often associated with poor oral hygiene, gingivitis, and
periodontal disease.

• Detection alone does not prove that the organism caused the disease; it may
colonize an already diseased oral environment.

• Rare reports describe it at extraoral sites, but these are not the typical
presentation and do not establish causality in every case.

• It is not considered a sexually transmitted infection

• Transmission through saliva or shared items contaminated with oral secretions
is plausible, but the importance of each route is not precisely defined.

• The moist oral environment, dental plaque, and periodontal pockets may
support its persistence.

[Slide 17]

T. tenax — Morphology and General Features

• It exists as a motile trophozoite; no cyst stage is known.

• It has anterior flagella and an undulating membrane, but
morphology may overlap with that of other trichomonads.

• Species identification based on morphology alone may be
difficult, particularly in suboptimal specimens.

• It inhabits moist oral environments and may be detected in
plaque or periodontal-pocket specimens using suitable methods.

• Molecular testing may be needed for precise identification in
research or selected cases.

[Slide 18]

T. tenax — Epidemiology and Pathogenicity

• Detection rates vary with oral health, the population studied, specimen collection, and
diagnostic method.

• It may be found more often in people with periodontal disease or poor oral hygiene.

• Its direct causal role in gingival inflammation or tissue destruction has not been fully established.

• Rare reports describe detection in respiratory specimens or abscesses; these findings should be
interpreted cautiously.

• There are no symptoms specific to T. tenax alone; clinical findings are usually related to the
underlying oral condition.

[Slide 19]

T. tenax — Laboratory Diagnosis

• Specimens may include dental plaque, periodontal-pocket material, or other appropriate oral
samples, depending on the purpose of testing.

• Direct microscopy of a fresh sample may reveal motile trophozoites, but morphology may not
reliably establish the species.

• Culture or molecular testing may be used in research laboratories or when species confirmation
is required.

• Detection must be distinguished from proof of causation: finding the organism does not by itself
establish that it caused periodontal disease.

• Clinical management focuses on assessment and treatment of oral disease and dental hygiene
by an appropriate professional.

TRICHOMONAS HOMINIS (intestinal)


[Slide 21]

3. Pentatrichomonas hominis — Name and Significance

• The organism is commonly called Pentatrichomonas hominis; older literature may refer to it as
Trichomonas hominis.

• It is an intestinal trichomonad found in the colon and passed in feces.

• It is generally considered nonpathogenic, although a possible role in diarrhea remains debated
and other pathogens may be present at the same time.

• Diarrhea should not automatically be attributed to this organism without investigating other
causes of intestinal infection.

• When teaching or reviewing literature, provide both the current name and the historical name
when useful.

[Slide 22]

P. hominis — Morphology and General Features

• The trophozoite is elongated or pear-shaped and moves using
flagella.

• It has anterior flagella and an undulating membrane; detailed
morphology should be checked against the diagnostic reference
used.

• No cyst stage is known; the form observed in stool is the
trophozoite.

• It may resemble other trichomonads, so morphology must be
interpreted alongside specimen origin and other microscopic
features.

• Trophozoites are relatively fragile; fresh stool is preferred
when motility is being assessed.

[Slide 23]

P. hominis — Epidemiology and Pathogenicity

• It is reported worldwide, with detection rates varying by sanitation, hygiene, and diagnostic
methods.

• Transmission is presumed to occur mainly by the fecal–oral route through contaminated hands,
food, or water.

• Most references consider it a commensal or an organism of uncertain/low pathogenicity; a
consistent causal relationship with diarrhea has not been established.

• When gastrointestinal symptoms are present, evaluate for better-established causes such as
Giardia, Entamoeba histolytica, and relevant bacterial or viral pathogens.

• It is not usually regarded as an invasive pathogen and does not require a complex life cycle or
intermediate host.

[Slide 24]

P. hominis — Life Cycle

• Trophozoites are passed in the feces of colonized individuals.

• Transmission is presumed to occur when fecally contaminated material is ingested.

• The organism reaches and inhabits the lumen of the large intestine.

• It reproduces by longitudinal binary fission, and trophozoites are passed again in stool.

• No resistant cyst stage is known, which likely limits environmental survival compared with
parasites that form resistant cysts.

[Slide 25]

P. hominis — Laboratory Diagnosis

• Specimen: fresh stool; prompt examination is
preferred when motility is being assessed.

• Direct wet-mount microscopy may reveal motile
trophozoites, but sensitivity depends on timing and
specimen quality.

• Concentration methods or permanent stains may
help clarify morphology according to the laboratory
protocol.

• Species-level identification can be difficult by
microscopy alone; molecular tests may help in
research or unclear cases.

• Detection in stool does not prove that P. hominis
caused symptoms; correlate with the clinical picture
and investigate other pathogens.

[Slide 26]

Laboratory Comparison of the Main Species

• T. vaginalis: urogenital tract; specimens include vaginal secretions/swabs or suitable urine and genital specimens; NAAT
is often the preferred confirmatory method.

• T. tenax: oral cavity and periodontal pockets; specimens include dental plaque or gingival material; microscopy or
molecular testing may be used depending on purpose.

• P. hominis: colon; specimen is fresh stool; direct microscopy may be used, with cautious interpretation of clinical
significance.

• Specimen source is essential for differentiation; motility or the name alone is insufficient.

[Slide 27]

Key Differences in Life Cycle and Transmission

• T. vaginalis: primarily sexual transmission; urogenital colonization; no known cyst stage.

• T. tenax: associated with the oral cavity; transmission through saliva or oral secretions is
plausible; no known cyst stage.

• P. hominis: intestinal habitat; presumed fecal–oral transmission; no known cyst stage.

• All species discussed reproduce by binary fission, but their habitats and transmission routes
differ.

[Slide 28]

Common Diagnostic Pitfalls

• Do not treat a negative wet mount as definitive exclusion of T. vaginalis; sensitivity is lower than
that of molecular tests.

• Do not delay wet-mount examination and then rely on absent motility to exclude the organism.

• Detection of T. tenax does not prove that it caused periodontal disease.

• Do not attribute diarrhea to P. hominis without considering other intestinal pathogens.

• Do not confuse the historical name Trichomonas hominis with the current name
Pentatrichomonas hominis.

[Slide 29]

Conclusion

• T. vaginalis is the most clinically important species and causes a treatable sexually transmitted
urogenital infection.

• T. tenax is associated with the oral cavity and periodontal disease, but its causal role is not
established in every case.

• P. hominis is an intestinal trichomonad generally considered nonpathogenic or of uncertain
pathogenicity.

• The main human-associated species discussed have no known cyst stage and reproduce by
binary fission.

• Appropriate specimen selection, prompt examination, and NAAT when available are important
for reliable diagnosis.
