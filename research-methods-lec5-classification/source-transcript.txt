Lecture Five

Classification of Researches

Research is a systematic and refined technique of thinking,
employing specialised tools, instruments and procedure in order to
obtain a more adequate solution of a problem. Research is, thus, a
systematic quest for undiscovered truth.

Research can be classified in several ways. The classification
depends on the purpose of the study, the research approach, the
objective, the source and type of data, the research design, the time
dimension, and the relationship between the study and existing
theories. A single research project can therefore belong to several
research categories at the same time.

A scientific research project commonly includes several stages:
identifying a research problem, reviewing previous studies,
developing research questions or hypotheses, selecting an
appropriate methodology, collecting data, analyzing the evidence,
interpreting the findings, and drawing conclusions.

The most useful classifications include purpose, approach,
objective, research design, time, data source, reasoning, and
relationship with theory.

  1. Basic or Fundamental Research

   Basic research, also called fundamental or theoretical research,
  is conducted primarily to increase knowledge and improve
  understanding of fundamental principles, processes, and
  phenomena. It may investigate questions about how or why
  something occurs without requiring an immediate practical
  application.

                                 1
Basic research is important because it builds the foundation for later
discoveries and applied research. Its findings may contribute to new
theories, concepts, explanations, or scientific knowledge.

Characteristics of basic research:

  a. Expands fundamental knowledge.
  b. Focuses on underlying principles and processes.
  c. Attempts to explain why phenomena occur.
  d. May develop or refine theories and concepts.
  e. Does not necessarily have an immediate practical application.
  f. Can provide a foundation for future applied research.

Mostly, Basic research is done in pure science like theoretical
physics, chemistry, biology and mathematics.

Examples:

       • Identification of new species (Viruses, bacteria, fungi,
          etc.)
  •   Embryology: Development of cell lines in the embryo. Allen

  •   Cell biology: How do cells divide, communicate, or respond
      to stress?
  •   Genetics: How do genes work.
  •   Immunology: How do immune cells recognize pathogens or
      distinguish them from the body’s own cells?
  •   Neuroscience: How do neural circuits support memory,

  •   Microbiology: How do bacteria and viruses grow, evolve,

  •   Physiology: How do organs regulate functions such as blood

  •   Mode of action of toxins.

  2. Applied Research

  Applied research is conducted to address a specific practical
  problem or need. It uses established knowledge, theories,
  methods, or principles to develop solutions, find new practices,
  improve practices, or support decision-making.

Applied research is common in agriculture, biology, medicine,
engineering, business, education, environmental science, and many
other fields. The outcome is often intended to have a practical
use.

Characteristics of applied research:

  a. Addresses a specific practical problem.
  b. Uses existing knowledge and established methods.
  c. Seeks useful and applicable solutions.
  d. Often has a direct connection with real-world needs.
  e. Can improve products, processes, policies, services, or
     practices.

Examples:

  •    Drug development: Testing compounds that could treat
      cancer, infections, or other diseases.
  •   Vaccine research: Designing and evaluating vaccines
      against specific pathogens.
  •   Medical diagnostics: Developing tests that detect diseases
      earlier or more accurately.
  •   Gene therapy: Investigating ways to correct or compensate
      for disease-causing genetic changes.
  •   Tissue engineering: Creating materials or methods to repair
      or replace damaged tissues.
  •   Tissue Culture: Creating tissues from single cell culture
      which maybe lead to producing high numbers of an important
      plant (e.g. Palm trees).

  •   Clinical research: Comparing treatments or care strategies
      to determine which work best for patients.

3. Quantitative Research

Quantitative research uses numerical data to measure variables,
examine patterns, compare groups, and test relationships or
hypotheses. Statistical and mathematical techniques are commonly
used to analyze the data.

Quantitative studies often present findings through tables, charts,
graphs, percentages, averages, statistical tests, and other numerical
summaries.

Uses numerical data. Measures variables in a structured way.
Frequently uses statistical analysis. Can be used to test hypotheses
and relationships. Findings are often presented in tables, charts, or
graphs.

4. Qualitative Research

Qualitative research focuses on meanings, experiences, perceptions,
behaviors, processes, and social or human phenomena. Instead of
primarily measuring variables numerically, it seeks a deeper
understanding of how people experience or interpret a situation.

Common qualitative data sources include interviews, observations,
focus groups, documents, field notes, and other descriptive
materials.

Uses mainly non-numerical or descriptive data. Explores meanings,
experiences, and perceptions. Often uses interviews, observations,
or document analysis. Helps explain how and why something
occurs. Is frequently exploratory in nature.

5. Mixed-Methods Research

                                  4
Mixed-methods research combines quantitative and qualitative
approaches within one research project. For example, a researcher
may use a questionnaire to collect numerical data and interviews to
understand participants’ experiences in greater depth.

This approach can provide a broader understanding of a research
problem when both numerical evidence and contextual information
are important.

6. Exploratory Research

Exploratory research is used when a topic, problem, or phenomenon
is not yet clearly understood. It helps researchers identify important
issues, variables, questions, and possible explanations.

Exploratory research may involve literature reviews, interviews,
focus groups, observations, or preliminary investigations. It often
helps researchers design a more focused study later.

7. Descriptive Research

Descriptive research aims to describe the characteristics, conditions,
behaviors, or properties of a population, group, event, or
phenomenon. It answers questions such as what, where, when, and
how much.

Descriptive research does not necessarily explain why something
happens. Its main purpose is to provide an accurate description of
the situation being studied.

8. Explanatory Research

Explanatory research seeks to understand relationships between
variables and explain why or how a phenomenon occurs. It may
investigate associations, mechanisms, or possible cause-and-effect
relationships.

                                  5
Explanatory studies often build on descriptive or exploratory
findings and seek deeper explanations.

9. Experimental Research

Experimental research involves deliberately manipulating one or
more variables and observing the resulting effects under controlled
conditions. It is especially useful when researchers want to
investigate cause-and-effect relationships.

Experimental designs are widely used in scientific fields, including
biology, agriculture, medicine, chemistry, and environmental
science.

10. Observational Research

Observational research involves systematically observing and
recording subjects, events, or phenomena without directly
manipulating the variables under study. It is useful when
experimentation is impractical, inappropriate, or unnecessary.

11. Case Study Research

Case study research provides an in-depth investigation of a
particular individual, group, organization, community, event,
process, or other defined case. It can combine multiple sources of
evidence and is useful for understanding complex real-world
situations.
