Lec. 1
Introduction to Human Immunology
From Thucydides' plague observations in 430 BC to CRISPR-engineered T cells in 2024 — the story of immunology is humanity's greatest detective story, unraveling a system of 37 trillion cells, 100 billion unique antibody sequences, and molecular signals that distinguish self from non-self with nanometer precision.
--- Slide 2
The Dawn of Immunity
430 BC
During the Athenian Plague, Thucydides recorded in his History of the Peloponnesian War that survivors developed resistance — they could care for the dying without contracting the disease a second time. This is the earliest written account of acquired immunity, predating germ theory by over 2,000 years.
15th Century
Chinese physicians of the Song Dynasty developed 'variolation' — grinding dried smallpox scabs into powder and blowing it into the nostrils of healthy children. Ottoman physicians refined this into cutaneous inoculation, inserting pus from mild cases under the skin. Mortality from variolation was ~1–2%, versus ~30% from natural smallpox infection.
1718
Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, wife of the British Ambassador to Constantinople, had her own children variolated and wrote detailed letters to London describing the procedure. She championed its adoption in Britain, leading to the first controlled human trial: six condemned prisoners at Newgate Prison were inoculated in 1721 in exchange for their freedom.
--- Slide 3
1796
The Jenner Breakthrough
Edward Jenner, a country physician in Berkeley, Gloucestershire, observed that milkmaid Sarah Nelmes had contracted cowpox (Vaccinia virus) from a cow named Blossom. On May 14, 1796, he inoculated 8-year-old James Phipps with material from Nelmes' lesions. Six weeks later, Jenner challenged Phipps with live smallpox — the boy showed no signs of infection.
Jenner coined the term vaccine from the Latin vacca (cow), and his 1798 paper An Inquiry into the Causes and Effects of the Variolae Vaccinae launched the era of modern immunization. The WHO declared smallpox eradicated in 1980 — the only human disease ever eliminated by vaccination.
--- Slide 4
1880S
Pasteur and the Germ Theory
Germ Theory
Pasteur's 1857 fermentation experiments disproved spontaneous generation, establishing that microorganisms — not "bad air" — cause disease. Infection became a biological battle between host and pathogen, overturning miasma theory and transforming medicine.
Attenuated Vaccines
In 1880, a forgotten chicken cholera culture had weakened in his lab — yet when injected, it protected chickens from fresh, virulent strains. Pasteur recognized attenuation as a universal principle, developing vaccines for fowl cholera (1880) and anthrax (1881), famously demonstrated at Pouilly-le-Fort before 200 witnesses.
Rabies, 1885
Pasteur's rabies vaccine saved 9-year-old Joseph Meister after a severe dog bite — the first successful post-exposure prophylaxis in history. The treatment required 13 inoculations over 10 days and marked the birth of the Institut Pasteur.
--- Slide 5
1883
Metchnikoff and Cellular Immunity
In 1882, Russian zoologist Élie Metchnikoff inserted a rose thorn into a starfish larva and watched, under his microscope, as mobile cells swarmed to engulf the foreign object. He named these cells phagocytes — from the Greek phagein, meaning "to eat."
Metchnikoff identified two distinct types: macrophages (large eaters) and microphages — later renamed neutrophils. His landmark 1883 paper proposed that these roving cellular defenders, not antibodies or chemical agents, formed the body's primary line of defense against infection — founding the field of cellular immunity.
His work stood in direct contrast to Paul Ehrlich's humoral theory, which emphasized antibodies. In 1908, both men shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, their rival theories ultimately recognized as complementary pillars of modern immunology.
--- Slide 6
1897
Ehrlich and Humoral Immunity
German physician Paul Ehrlich proposed the Side-Chain Theory in 1897 — that cells carry pre-formed surface receptors (side-chains) that bind specific antigens like a lock and key. When a side-chain bound an antigen, the cell shed it into the blood as a soluble antibody and produced more.
Ehrlich mapped five antibody classes and coined the term "magic bullet" (Zauberkugel) — a molecule that could target pathogens without harming the host. His 1909 discovery of Salvarsan (compound 606), an arsenic-based treatment for syphilis, was the first modern chemotherapy.
Ehrlich's receptor theory directly predicted the clonal selection theory proposed by Burnet in 1957, cementing his legacy as a founding architect of humoral immunity.
--- Slide 7
1900
Landsteiner and Blood Groups
In 1901, Austrian immunologist Karl Landsteiner discovered the ABO blood group system by mixing blood samples from his colleagues and observing agglutination patterns. He identified three groups — A, B, and O — with a fourth (AB) described by his students in 1902. The discovery explained why early blood transfusions often killed patients: mismatched antigens on red blood cells triggered a fatal immune response. Landsteiner also discovered the Rh factor in 1937 (with Alexander Wiener), explaining hemolytic disease of the newborn. His work earned the 1930 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine and made modern surgery, trauma care, and organ transplantation possible. Today, blood typing remains one of the most routine immunological tests performed worldwide.
ABO System
A, B, AB, and O groups defined by surface glycoproteins.
Clinical Impact
Safe blood transfusion became possible, saving countless lives.
Nobel Prize
Landsteiner was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1930.
--- Slide 8
PART II
The Architecture of Defense
The human immune system is a distributed network of ~2 trillion lymphocytes, 100 billion antibody variants, and dozens of specialized cell types operating across every tissue in the body. It is organized into two interlocking branches: the Innate Immune System — ancient, fast-acting, and non-specific — and the Adaptive Immune System — slower, exquisitely specific, and capable of immunological memory. The innate system responds within minutes to hours; the adaptive system takes 7–14 days on first exposure but can respond within hours on re-exposure. Together, they form a layered defense that distinguishes self from non-self, tolerates commensal microbiota, and eliminates pathogens, cancer cells, and damaged tissue.
Innate Immunity
Rapid, non-specific first response. No memory. Present from birth.
Adaptive Immunity
Slow, highly specific second response. Forms immunological memory.
--- Slide 9
Innate Immunity: The First Line
The innate immune system is the body's immediate, non-specific defense — active within seconds to minutes of pathogen encounter. It relies on Pattern Recognition Receptors (PRRs) — including Toll-Like Receptors (TLRs), NOD-like receptors, and RIG-I-like receptors — to detect conserved microbial structures called Pathogen-Associated Molecular Patterns (PAMPs), such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) on gram-negative bacteria, flagellin, and double-stranded RNA from viruses.
Physical Barriers
Skin (pH 4.5–5.5, antimicrobial peptides), mucus membranes (containing lysozyme and secretory IgA), and the ciliated epithelium of the respiratory tract physically block and expel pathogens before infection can establish.
The Complement System
A cascade of 30+ plasma proteins that can lyse pathogens directly via the Membrane Attack Complex (MAC) within minutes of activation — opsonizing targets, recruiting phagocytes, and amplifying the inflammatory response.
No Memory
Innate responses are universal and immediate — but structurally identical on every exposure. PAMPs are recognized by germline-encoded receptors, not clonally selected ones, meaning the system cannot adapt or improve with repeated infection.
--- Slide 10
The Innate Cellular Arsenal
Neutrophils
The most abundant white blood cell (~60% of circulating leukocytes). First responders to bacterial infection, arriving at sites of inflammation within 30–60 minutes via chemotaxis. They kill pathogens through phagocytosis, degranulation (releasing elastase and myeloperoxidase), and Neutrophil Extracellular Traps (NETs) — webs of DNA and antimicrobial proteins that ensnare and destroy pathogens. Lifespan: 6–8 hours in circulation.
Macrophages
Derived from circulating monocytes, macrophages are long-lived tissue-resident sentinels — Kupffer cells in the liver, microglia in the brain, alveolar macrophages in the lung. They phagocytose pathogens, present antigens via MHC II, and secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6). A single macrophage can engulf up to 100 bacteria.
Natural Killer (NK) Cells
NK cells patrol for cells that have downregulated MHC I — a hallmark of viral infection and malignancy. They kill via perforin/granzyme B injection and Fas-FasL signaling, without prior sensitization. NK cells account for 5–15% of circulating lymphocytes and are critical in early antiviral defense — acting before the adaptive immune response is established.
--- Slide 11
The Inflammatory Response
Inflammation is the innate immune system's emergency broadcast — a coordinated vascular and cellular response to tissue damage or infection. Within seconds of injury, mast cells and macrophages release histamine and prostaglandins, causing vasodilation and increased vascular permeability. The four cardinal signs — redness (rubor), heat (calor), swelling (tumor), and pain (dolor) — were described by Celsus in 30 AD. At the molecular level, NF-κB activation drives transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines: TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-8. Neutrophils extravasate through the endothelium via selectin-integrin interactions (rolling → tethering → firm adhesion → diapedesis) and arrive at the infection site within 30–60 minutes. Unresolved inflammation — driven by persistent NF-κB signaling — underlies atherosclerosis, type 2 diabetes, Alzheimer's disease, and cancer.

--- Slide 12
The Lymphoid System
The lymphoid system is the anatomical infrastructure of adaptive immunity — a network of primary and secondary lymphoid organs connected by lymphatic vessels carrying ~4 liters of lymph per day.
Bone marrow: Site of B cell development and all hematopoiesis — every blood cell lineage originates here
Thymus: Where T cells undergo rigorous positive and negative selection and maturation before peripheral deployment
Lymph nodes: ~600 nodes throughout the body serve as antigen surveillance hubs, filtering lymph and initiating adaptive responses
Spleen: Filters ~500 mL of blood per minute, removes opsonized pathogens, and houses ~25% of the body's total lymphocyte pool
MALT: Mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue — including Peyer's patches in the gut, tonsils, and bronchus-associated lymphoid tissue (BALT) — defends mucosal surfaces

PART III
The Adaptive Revolution
The adaptive immune system is evolution's masterpiece of specificity — capable of generating over 1018 unique antigen receptors through V(D)J recombination, a process of random gene segment shuffling that creates diversity exceeding the number of atoms in the observable universe. Unlike innate immunity, the adaptive system requires 7–14 days to mount a primary response — but forms immunological memory that enables a secondary response within 24–72 hours. The two key cell types are B lymphocytes (which produce antibodies) and T lymphocytes (which kill infected cells and coordinate the response). Both originate in the bone marrow; T cells migrate to the thymus for maturation. The adaptive system is the basis of all vaccines, immunotherapies, and organ transplant medicine.
01
Clonal Selection Theory (1957)
Burnet proposed that each lymphocyte carries a unique antigen receptor, selected and expanded only when its specific antigen is encountered.
02
Clonal Expansion
Activated lymphocytes proliferate rapidly, generating thousands of identical effector cells targeted at a single antigen.
03
Immunological Memory
A subset of expanded clones persists as long-lived memory cells, enabling a faster and stronger response upon re-exposure.
--- Slide 14
Antigen Presentation: The Critical Handshake
Dendritic cells (DCs) — the most potent antigen-presenting cells (APCs) — sample peripheral tissues, engulf pathogens, and process proteins into short peptide fragments (8–25 amino acids). Upon activation, DCs upregulate CCR7 and migrate to draining lymph nodes, where they present antigen to naïve T cells. This process is the critical link between innate and adaptive immunity. The human MHC locus (HLA) is the most polymorphic region of the human genome, encoding over 28,000 known alleles. Remarkably, DCs can also perform cross-presentation — loading exogenous antigens onto MHC Class I — which is essential for anti-tumor and antiviral immunity.
MHC Class I
Present on all nucleated cells. Loads endogenous (intracellular) peptide fragments — derived from cytoplasmic proteins or viral replication — and presents them to CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells, triggering targeted killing of infected or malignant cells.
MHC Class II
Restricted to professional APCs (dendritic cells, macrophages, B cells). Presents exogenous (extracellular) antigens processed via the endolysosomal pathway to CD4⁺ helper T cells, orchestrating the broader adaptive immune response.
--- Slide 15
B Cells and Humoral Immunity
Maturation
B cells develop in the bone marrow through ordered stages: pro-B → pre-B → immature B → mature naïve B cell. V(D)J recombination at the immunoglobulin heavy chain locus (chromosome 14) and light chain loci (chromosomes 2 and 22) generates a unique B-cell receptor (BCR). Cells that bind self-antigens too strongly undergo receptor editing or apoptosis (central tolerance).
Activation
Naïve B cells in lymph node follicles encounter antigen via their BCR. T-dependent activation requires CD4⁺ T helper cell co-stimulation (CD40–CD40L interaction + cytokine signals). T-independent antigens (e.g., bacterial polysaccharides) can activate B cells directly. Activated B cells form germinal centers — specialized microstructures where somatic hypermutation and affinity maturation occur over 7–21 days.
Differentiation
Germinal center B cells differentiate into plasma cells (antibody factories producing up to 10,000 antibodies/second) or long-lived memory B cells. Class switching (IgM → IgG, IgA, IgE) is driven by cytokine signals: IL-4 → IgE, TGF-β → IgA, IFN-γ → IgG2a.
Antibody Function
The five immunoglobulin classes (IgM, IgG, IgA, IgE, IgD) have distinct effector functions: IgG neutralizes pathogens and crosses the placenta; IgA protects mucosal surfaces; IgE mediates allergy and anti-parasitic responses; IgM is the first antibody produced in a primary response.
--- Slide 16
T Cells and Cell-Mediated Immunity
CD4⁺ Helper T Cells
The conductors of adaptive immunity. Th1 cells (IFN-γ, TNF-α) drive macrophage activation and cellular immunity; Th2 cells (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13) drive B cell responses and allergy; Th17 cells (IL-17) defend against extracellular bacteria and fungi; Tfh cells (CXCR5⁺, IL-21) support germinal center reactions.
CD8⁺ Cytotoxic T Cells
Precision executioners that kill infected and tumor cells by injecting perforin and granzyme B, inducing apoptosis within minutes of contact. Activation requires Signal 1 (TCR–MHC/peptide) and Signal 2 (CD28–B7 co-stimulation). Without Signal 2, T cells become anergic.
Thymic Development & Selection
T cells develop in the thymus, where ~98% of precursors are eliminated through two checkpoints: positive selection (must recognize self-MHC) and negative selection (must not react to self-peptides). The surviving 2% exit as naïve T cells expressing either CD4 or CD8 co-receptors, ready to patrol the periphery for foreign antigens presented on MHC molecules.
--- Slide 17
The Memory Advantage
Immunological memory is the molecular basis of vaccination. After a primary immune response, ~5–10% of activated B and T cells survive as long-lived memory cells — some persisting for decades or even a lifetime.
Memory B cells express higher-affinity BCRs (due to somatic hypermutation) and class-switched antibodies. Memory T cells are subdivided into three populations:
Central Memory T cells (TCM, CCR7⁺, CD62L⁺) — recirculate through lymph nodes and are poised for rapid expansion
Effector Memory T cells (TEM, CCR7⁻) — patrol peripheral tissues and can kill immediately upon re-encounter
Tissue-Resident Memory T cells (TRM, CD69⁺, CD103⁺) — permanently reside in tissues like the lung, gut, and skin
On secondary exposure, the response is 100–1,000× faster and produces 10–100× more antibody, predominantly IgG (vs. IgM in the primary response). This is why a second vaccine dose produces dramatically higher antibody titers.
--- Slide 18
PART IV
When the System Fails
The immune system's extraordinary power comes with extraordinary risk. When it fails — through deficiency, misdirection, or overactivation — the consequences are severe.
Immunodeficiency
Ranges from SCID ('bubble boy disease') — caused by mutations in the IL-2Rγ chain or ADA gene, leaving infants with virtually no functional T or B cells — to HIV/AIDS, which depletes CD4⁺ T cells below 200 cells/μL (normal: 500–1,500), collapsing adaptive immunity entirely.
Autoimmunity
Affects ~8% of the global population. In rheumatoid arthritis, autoreactive T cells and antibodies attack synovial joints. In type 1 diabetes, CD8⁺ T cells destroy pancreatic beta cells. In SLE, anti-dsDNA antibodies form immune complexes depositing in kidneys, joints, and skin.
Hypersensitivity
Classified by Gell-Coombs Types I–IV: Type I (IgE-mediated) drives anaphylaxis and hay fever; Type II targets cell-surface antigens; Type III involves immune complex deposition; Type IV is delayed-type hypersensitivity (e.g., tuberculin skin test), mediated by T cells rather than antibodies.
--- Slide 19
The Challenge of Self-Tolerance
Self-tolerance is enforced by two overlapping mechanisms: central tolerance (in the thymus and bone marrow) and peripheral tolerance (in tissues and lymph nodes). Breakdown of any of these mechanisms can trigger autoimmunity.
1
Central Tolerance & Thymic Selection
In the thymus, the AIRE (Autoimmune Regulator) gene drives expression of peripheral tissue antigens — including insulin, thyroglobulin, and myelin — enabling negative selection to delete self-reactive T cells before they reach circulation. AIRE mutations cause APECED (Autoimmune Polyendocrinopathy-Candidiasis-Ectodermal Dystrophy), a rare multi-organ autoimmune disease.
2
Peripheral Tolerance Mechanisms
Self-reactive cells that escape the thymus are controlled by three overlapping mechanisms: anergy (T cells receiving Signal 1 without costimulatory Signal 2 become permanently unresponsive); regulatory T cells (Tregs, CD4⁺CD25⁺Foxp3⁺) that actively suppress autoreactive lymphocytes; and activation-induced cell death (AICD) via Fas–FasL signaling, eliminating chronically activated self-reactive clones.
--- Slide 20
PART V
The Regulatory Network
The immune system is not simply an on/off switch — it is a finely tuned regulatory network that must calibrate responses to be strong enough to eliminate pathogens but restrained enough to avoid collateral damage. Key regulatory mechanisms include:
Checkpoint Molecules
CTLA-4 competes with CD28 for B7 binding, delivering an inhibitory signal that dampens T cell activation. PD-1 binds PD-L1/PD-L2 on tumor cells and chronically infected cells, inducing T cell exhaustion and functional silencing.
Anti-Inflammatory Cytokines
IL-10, produced by Tregs and macrophages, suppresses pro-inflammatory TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-12. TGF-β inhibits T cell proliferation and actively promotes Treg differentiation, reinforcing peripheral tolerance.
Resolution of Inflammation
Lipoxins, resolvins, and protectins — derived from omega-3 fatty acids — actively terminate the inflammatory response and promote tissue repair, shifting the tissue from destruction back toward homeostasis.
Checkpoint Blockade in Cancer
Dysregulation of these checkpoints is exploited in cancer immunotherapy: anti-PD-1 (pembrolizumab) and anti-CTLA-4 (ipilimumab) "release the brakes" on anti-tumor T cells, restoring cytotoxic function against malignant cells.
--- Slide 21
The Art of Control: Regulatory T Cells
Regulatory T cells (Tregs) are the immune system's peacekeepers — a specialized CD4⁺ T cell subset defined by expression of the transcription factor Foxp3 (encoded on the X chromosome; mutations cause IPEX syndrome — Immune dysregulation, Polyendocrinopathy, Enteropathy, X-linked). Tregs constitute ~5–10% of peripheral CD4⁺ T cells and suppress immune responses through four key mechanisms:
IL-10 & TGF-β secretion — directly inhibiting effector T cells and antigen-presenting cells (APCs)
IL-2 consumption — Tregs express high-affinity CD25 (IL-2Rα) and deprive effector T cells of the essential survival cytokine IL-2
CTLA-4 expression — Tregs constitutively express CTLA-4, outcompeting CD28 for B7 ligands on APCs and dampening co-stimulation
Granzyme B-mediated cytolysis — Tregs can directly kill effector T cells and B cells via cytotoxic granule release
Treg dysfunction is implicated in autoimmune diseases, cancer immune evasion, and transplant rejection.
--- Slide 22
The Language of Immunity: Cytokines and Chemokines
Interleukins
Over 40 interleukins (IL-1 through IL-41+) coordinate cell-to-cell communication. IL-2 drives T cell proliferation; IL-4 and IL-13 promote Th2 responses and IgE class switching; IL-6 is the primary inducer of acute-phase proteins (CRP, fibrinogen) in the liver; IL-12 polarizes naïve T cells toward Th1; IL-17 recruits neutrophils to mucosal surfaces. Dysregulated IL-6 signaling drives cytokine release syndrome (CRS) in severe COVID-19 and CAR-T therapy.
Interferons
Type I interferons (IFN-α and IFN-β) are produced within hours of viral infection, inducing an antiviral state in neighboring cells by upregulating ISGs (Interferon-Stimulated Genes), including MX1, OAS1, and IFIT1. IFN-γ (Type II) is produced by Th1 and NK cells and is the master activator of macrophages. Type III interferons (IFN-λ) protect mucosal surfaces. Defects in IFN signaling (e.g., STAT1 mutations) cause life-threatening susceptibility to viral and mycobacterial infections.
Tumor Necrosis Factors
TNF-α, produced by macrophages and T cells, is a pleiotropic cytokine that induces fever, promotes neutrophil recruitment, and can trigger apoptosis via TNFR1. Chronic TNF-α overproduction drives the pathology of rheumatoid arthritis, Crohn's disease, and psoriasis. Anti-TNF biologics (etanercept, adalimumab, infliximab) are among the best-selling drugs in history, with combined annual sales exceeding $40 billion.
Chemokines
A family of ~50 small (8–10 kDa) chemoattractant cytokines that direct leukocyte migration via G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs). CXCL8 (IL-8) recruits neutrophils; CCL2 (MCP-1) recruits monocytes; CXCL10 (IP-10) attracts activated T cells; CXCL12 (SDF-1) retains hematopoietic stem cells in bone marrow. CCR5 and CXCR4 are co-receptors for HIV-1 entry — individuals with the CCR5-Δ32 deletion are naturally resistant to HIV infection.
--- Slide 23
PART VI
The Modern Frontier
The 21st century has transformed immunology from a descriptive science into a precision engineering discipline. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) can now profile the transcriptome of individual immune cells at scale — revealing previously unknown cell subtypes and activation states. Mass cytometry (CyTOF) simultaneously measures 40+ protein markers per cell, mapping the full immune landscape of a tumor or tissue. CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing enables precise modification of immune cell genes — knocking out PD-1 in CAR-T cells to prevent exhaustion, or inserting synthetic antigen receptors. The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated mRNA vaccine technology (BNT162b2, mRNA-1273) from concept to 3 billion doses in under 18 months — a feat that would have taken decades with traditional platforms. Immune checkpoint inhibitors have transformed oncology: 5-year survival in metastatic melanoma has risen from ~5% (2010) to ~50% (2024) with combination anti-PD-1/anti-CTLA-4 therapy.
--- Slide 24
Precision Medicine: Monoclonal Antibodies
Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) are engineered proteins designed to bind a single, specific molecular target with nanomolar affinity. Since the first therapeutic mAb (muromonab-CD3, 1986), over 100 mAbs have been FDA-approved. They are produced by fusing antibody-secreting B cells with immortal myeloma cells (hybridoma technology, Köhler & Milstein, 1975 Nobel Prize). Modern mAbs are fully humanized to minimize immunogenicity. Bispecific antibodies (e.g., blinatumomab) simultaneously bind CD3 on T cells and CD19 on B cell tumors, redirecting cytotoxic T cells to kill cancer.
Autoimmunity & Anti-TNF
Adalimumab (Humira) blocks TNF-alpha, halting the inflammatory cascade in rheumatoid arthritis, Crohn's disease, and psoriasis — the world's best-selling drug for 9 consecutive years at ~$20B/year. Anti-CD20 rituximab depletes B cells in lymphomas and autoimmune conditions.
Oncology & Checkpoint Blockade
Trastuzumab (Herceptin) targets HER2+ breast cancer; pembrolizumab (Keytruda) and nivolumab (Opdivo) unleash anti-tumor immunity via anti-PD-1 checkpoint blockade across multiple solid tumors.
Angiogenesis
Bevacizumab (Avastin) starves tumors by inhibiting VEGF-mediated blood vessel formation — blocking the tumor's ability to recruit new vasculature and cutting off its nutrient supply.
--- Slide 25
The Future: Engineering the Immune Response
CAR-T & Next-Generation Cell Therapy
Tisagenlecleucel (Kymriah) engineers a patient's own T cells to express synthetic receptors targeting tumor antigens, achieving complete remission in 81% of relapsed/refractory B-ALL patients. Next-generation CAR-T cells incorporate logic gates (AND/OR/NOT) to target multiple antigens simultaneously, dramatically reducing off-tumor toxicity.
mRNA & Neoantigen Vaccines
mRNA platforms can now encode any antigen within weeks of sequence identification. Personalized neoantigen vaccines — targeting tumor-specific mutations — are currently in Phase II trials for melanoma and pancreatic cancer, harnessing the immune system to recognize and destroy each patient's unique tumor fingerprint.
CRISPR, Gene Delivery & AI Design
CRISPR base editing can correct single-nucleotide mutations in hematopoietic stem cells — trials for sickle cell disease (CTX001) have achieved functional cures. Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) enable in vivo immune reprogramming without ex vivo manipulation. The convergence of AlphaFold, synthetic biology, and immunology is making the design of entirely novel receptors, cytokines, and vaccines a computational problem.
